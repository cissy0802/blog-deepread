Fourteen Years of Translating Character into Parameters

He spent fourteen years as an angel investor and concluded that most of his own track record was not his doing. Then he took the move that got him there — stop explaining things by character, go find the knob — and turned it on meditation and awakening.

Glossary

  • Yogacara — the Buddhist school founded by the brothers Asanga and Vasubandhu in fourth-century India: the world you experience is rendered by consciousness, and that rendering system has a detailed schematic. It is mainly what Xuanzang carried back from India.
  • alaya-vijnana / manas — the eighth and seventh consciousnesses: the first is the storehouse holding the "seeds" left by everything you have ever done; the second is the function that keeps claiming things out of that storehouse as "me" and "mine".
  • The three natures — three views of one event: paratantra (the raw dependently-arisen stream), parikalpita (the story you lay on top of it), parinispanna (how it stands when that story stops running).
  • Predictive coding / precision weighting — the brain guesses a world first and checks it against sensory data second. Every prior belief carries a weight for how certain the brain is of it; the higher the weight, the less data can shift it.

In September 2025 an angel investor who has backed more than three hundred companies wrote down a formula, and then used it to prove that the money he had made over the years had almost nothing to do with his judgement.

The formula is probability × multiple ≥ 10. A 1% chance at 1000x is 10, worth doing. A 1% chance at 100x is 1 — back a hundred companies, one hits, it returns a hundredfold, and the whole book has exactly broken even. He calls that working for nothing.

The problem is in the next line: where does the multiple come from?

What investors are eating is usually the soup that a rising tide, a trend everyone believes in, has served them... Investors make money on bubbles. No bubble, no excess return.— The Mathematical Essence of Investing, 2025-09-04

He is saying this about himself. In the same post he lays out the record: "Even in the best of times, out of 300 companies invested in, only 7 succeeded." And back in 2012, at the very start, he had already written that early-stage investing is at least 70% luck. Fourteen years later he turned that hunch into a formula and then acted on it: he used to make forty investments a year; over the last two years he has made four, moved to Japan, and mostly stopped looking at new deals.

A month later the blog changed. In its first fourteen years it carried 36 posts; since October 2025 it has carried 779. The subject matter changed too — from the Fed, the Shiller P/E and the yen carry trade to the storehouse consciousness, Nagarjuna, and the Surangama Sutra.

But the move is identical. For fourteen years he has done one thing: take a phenomenon people explain by character, and re-explain it by parameters.

Why does sitting meditation work? Not sincerity. Deep meditation lowers the precision weighting on high-level priors — the "I have to succeed", "people have to approve of me" beliefs whose weights are normally so high that neither sensory data nor reasoning can get through. Practice does not delete them; it turns the inflated ones back down. In Yogacara terms, clinging just is excess weight: it is not that your willpower is too weak, it is that the knob is stuck.

The move worked so well that he thought for a while it worked on everything. He gave awakening a definition he was rather pleased with: awakening is the deliberate rewriting of the parameters that govern your own consciousness — practice being a way to route around the firewall of self-clinging and edit the parameters directly.

Then a reader in the comments knocked him flat:

The seventh consciousness is not something you route around; it is something you see through. Who is it that wants to route around it and edit the parameters? There is still a router and a thing routed around — that is the seventh consciousness at work.— reader "Huang Jushi", quoted in The Concepts I Got Wrong, 2026-03-05

He went back and counted, and found that all four concepts he had botched failed in the same place: every one of his explanations had an "I" sitting comfortably at the centre of it. Which is precisely the last and subtlest layer of clinging Yogacara describes — you think you have broken it, but you have only upgraded it, from "the boss frowned, so I'm being fired" to "I am observing things as they are." The story got classier; the structure of someone telling a story never moved.

Put differently: the parameter story is also a story, and it too grows into something to cling to. The last line of that confession is the best sentence on the site —

I am not a teacher on this road. I am a man climbing the mountain who happens to have a microphone.— The Concepts I Got Wrong, 2026-03-05

01Something odd

On 4 September 2025, an angel investor with more than three hundred companies behind him wrote a formula on his blog: probability × multiple ≥ 10.

A 1% shot at 1000x is 10, worth doing. A 1% shot at 100x is 1 — a hundred bets, one hit, a hundredfold return, and the book has exactly broken even. He calls that working for nothing.

Then he put himself into it: "Even in the best of times, out of 300 companies invested in, only 7 succeeded."

Using a formula to argue that the core skill of your own profession does not matter much is a poor trade. He wrote it anyway, and then acted on it.

02Who he is

Wang Lijie, who goes by Leo. Out of Huawei, quit in 2007 to go it alone, and watched his own social-network startup die. In 2011 he took nearly all the cash he had — fifteen thousand dollars — for 2% of an engineer community, and called himself the poorest angel investor alive. One founder he passed on messaged him back: "If you don't have money, don't invest, fool."

He went on to become founding partner of PreAngel, with three hundred-odd investments. After the pandemic he moved his family abroad, sat the TOEFL five times in a year to score 101, and recently quit smoking.

The blog has existed since 2011 and carried 36 posts in its first fourteen years. Since October 2025 it has gone daily and now runs to 779 — it is the text arm of the MacroAlpha video channel, whose pipeline he built himself over half a year with Claude Code and Antigravity, one man shipping 45 episodes a month. It was still publishing the day this page was written.

03What he keeps fighting

One root question he has not put down in fourteen years: given one lifetime each, why do people's outputs differ so enormously?

On New Year's Day 2017 he wrote a letter to the PreAngel partners called Pain Is For Evolution. The answer there is the "cognitive model": lifespans differ by at most a hundredfold, achievements by tens of thousands, and the gap sits in the decision algorithm. Every prescription is additive — deliberate practice, 1% better every day, exchange energy with the world to generate negative entropy, and once you see the direction, go all in. It closes: "Don't die in pain, rise in pain. The more pain, the more progress."

His own profession then took that answer apart. Over a decade of early-stage investing he kept hitting the same thing: people of comparable ability end up wildly apart, and the explanation does not live inside them. In 2012 he said it was at least 70% luck; in 2025 he supplied the remaining 30% — you make your money on bubbles.

So he spent fourteen years carefully establishing that personal quality does not explain outcomes. What does, then? He went looking for the underlying parameters — and that search walked him out of finance and into Yogacara.

04His ideas, told the way he found them

1. Fourteen years spent proving his own record mostly wasn't his doing

In 2012 the logic was still plain: small cheques, many of them, fifty companies a year, a decision after an hour or two of conversation, almost no diligence, on the grounds that early-stage investing is investing in people. Yet in the same piece he said the thing successful people do not say:

Investment and early-stage investment are at least 70% luck, and only 30% rely on your efforts. In the end, it is luck.— The Poorest Angel Investor, 2012-03-10

Thirteen years later he opened that sentence up. The remaining 30% is not insight, it is whether you were standing on a bubble: hit rates are set by the macro environment and consumer demand, neither of which is yours to control; multiples are set by how inflated your sector is — when everybody believes in a direction, a bubble forms, a bubble means someone will take the next round, and only that produces excess return.

So the famous eye for a deal is mostly, in his words, soup served up by the tide.

2. Meditation isn't mysticism; it's turning down a knob called precision weighting

He starts a piece on meditation not with meditation but with an anatomical fact: of the synaptic input arriving at primary visual cortex, only a tiny fraction comes from the retina — the overwhelming majority comes from higher regions of the brain itself. "Your brain spends far more energy talking downward than it spends listening upward." The brain is not a camera; it is a prediction generator.

The key move is next: every prior belief carries a precision weight. Then he lays Yogacara's three natures on top — not as a simile, but layer by layer:

Once they line up, clinging gets an operational definition for the first time: to cling is for a prior's precision to be set so high that the correction signal from the senses cannot get through. As he writes: "This explains why some people plainly see the truth and still can't change: it isn't that their willpower is insufficient, it's that the weight on that prior is too high."

From there he picks up Friston's free energy principle: the core task of a living system is to minimise surprise. Isn't that just seeking pleasure and avoiding pain? But the Buddha went one step further —

The root of suffering is not prediction error itself, but your attachment to eliminating prediction error... In a universe that is fundamentally uncertain, you are chasing an illusion of certainty.— What Is Your Brain Doing When You Sit?, 2026-02-25

Here is something that stings once you put the two side by side: in 2017 he was also using thermodynamics to talk about life — entropy is decay, negative entropy is vitality, so keep trading energy with the world and the more it hurts the more you grow. In 2026 he is using the same vocabulary and arrives at the reverse: the system frantically minimising surprise is itself the suffering.

3. The translation method hit something it couldn't translate — and a reader is who told him

It worked so well he thought it worked on everything. He gave awakening a definition he was pleased with: awakening is "the deliberate rewriting of the parameters that govern one's own consciousness", and the essence of every practice is to route around the firewall of self-clinging and edit those parameters. His own description of writing it: "It felt great — the Buddhist framework and the AI vocabulary mapped seamlessly onto each other."

A reader signing himself Huang Jushi left several comments. One of them knocked him flat:

The seventh consciousness is not something you route around; it is something you see through. Who is it that wants to route around it and edit the parameters? There is still a router and a thing routed around — that is still ignorance in the seventh consciousness at work. Only by seeing that the seventh consciousness was illusory all along...— reader Huang Jushi, quoted in The Concepts I Got Wrong, 2026-03-05

The moment you say "route around", you have assumed the wall is really there — and what Yogacara says is that the wall is a fabrication. He went back and counted: all four of the concepts he had been corrected on failed in the same place. Every explanation had an "I" sitting comfortably at the centre of it. That "I" changed outfits several times but never left. Which is the deepest and best-hidden layer of clinging there is.

So the conclusion flipped from addition to subtraction — awakening is not getting admin rights, it is peeling away one layer of unnecessary tension after another until what was always there shows itself.

I did not make these mistakes out of laziness. On the contrary — I researched them conscientiously, and then got them conscientiously wrong... It is like climbing: I am the guide, but this is my first time up the mountain too.— The Concepts I Got Wrong, 2026-03-05

05How he thinks

Find the knob that turns. To take apart something "mystical", his first move is never to ask whether it is true, but to ask which quantity in it can vary. Investing goes to bubble size; meditation to precision weighting; fate to how many seeds are in the store. He does not argue with a claim about whether it is right; he goes straight for its control panel.

He writes his readers' objections into the body of the essay, whole. At least two posts exist entirely because of the comments. A Huayan practitioner pointed out that he had drawn the sources of correction too narrowly — in dreams the external senses are off, but the brain's internal constraint on narrative coherence is still running. He does not write "thanks for the correction" and carry on; he quotes the objection at length, names the person, and re-argues the whole thing from the start.

06Where he may be wrong

Isomorphism comes too cheap. He declares in every piece that structural isomorphism is not equivalence, but he taps the brakes very lightly. On this blog the storehouse consciousness maps to parameter space, dharani to compression algorithms, Pure Land recitation to parameter optimisation. There is hardly anything the mapping cannot be made to fit — and a framework that explains everything usually predicts nothing.

In one place he breaks his own rule. His standing move is to reduce the mysterious to a mechanism. The piece on the ceiling of predictive coding runs the other way: predictive coding cannot account for Ramanujan's four thousand results, so he brings back seeds carried across lifetimes. He flags it as conjecture rather than settled — but the direction of inference is the reverse of everything else he writes. Elsewhere: the mystical turns out to have a mechanism. Here: the mechanism runs out, so something more mystical is called back in.

He hasn't turned his own ruler around on himself. He offers three tests for telling practice from coercive control: does it encourage you to leave; is your social world widening or narrowing; is there anyone who cannot be questioned. The third he probably passes. The first two deserve an answer from him: a daily-publishing cadence plus a paywalled members' tier plus a dedicated AI practice companion is a closed loop, and closed loops grow structurally in the direction of keeping you inside.

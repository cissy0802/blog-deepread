Tanner Greer
Acting together is a learned skill, and America is forgetting it

Tanner Greer · The Scholar’s Stage · essayist and independent researcher, director of the Center for Strategic Translation · blogging since 2009, still active (latest post at time of writing: 2026-09-19) · Corpus: about 600 posts by the site’s own post API; densest in 2010 (132 posts); in early 2011 he left for a two-year church mission and the blog stayed dark until February 2013; in 2022 he set up the translation center and output dropped sharply, to 4–11 longer pieces a year from 2023 to 2026; he says he deleted his very earliest posts as too embarrassing · This page is based on 18 posts actually read, spanning 2010–2026.

When the 1918 flu reached Illinois, the state immediately pulled together an Influenza Commission: the state and city health departments, the U.S. Public Health Service, the Red Cross, the military. It met nearly every day until the worst had passed. Pennsylvania carved itself into nineteen Epidemic Emergency Districts and drafted in “Health, Patriotic, Civic, Religious, Business and Social organizations.” When the epidemic ebbed, the committees dissolved.

When COVID arrived in 2020, nothing like that happened. Tanner Greer set the two years side by side and decided that explaining the failure piece by piece (an inept White House, sclerotic agencies, partisan warfare) was not enough, because every level failed: the White House, state and city governments, business, the press, the big civic associations. A national failure needs a national explanation, and he called it “culture.” His conclusion fits in one sentence:

In the 21st century, the main question in American social life is not “how do we make that happen?” but “how do we get management to take our side?”On Cultures That Build, 2020-06-18

“Management,” for Greer, does not just mean government. It means every vast institution you cannot reach and can only petition: the federal government, the conglomerates, the university administration, and the crowd of strangers on social media. His point is that petitioning management is a learned response, and today’s American elites never got the chance to learn any other.

His example is a thirty-year-old congressional staffer. Her youth was a long slog of tasks and awards accumulated to win over admissions committees; the politics she learned at university amounted to making a ruckus until the administration gave in. She learned one lesson: solving a problem means appealing upward.

He had been chasing this idea for years. In 2015 he took up the sociologists Campbell and Manning’s three “moral cultures.” In an honor culture you avenge an insult yourself. In a dignity culture you shrug it off, or go to court sparingly. In a victimhood culture you publicize your grievance and ask a powerful third party to step in. What Greer added was that these cultures are not just ideas: they are shadows of social structure. When the clans dissolved, honor could not hold. When local associations dissolved and everyone was atomized, facing only distant bureaucracies, the most effective move was to be the unambiguous victim.

His remedy is not romantic. Old America was not a nation of lone cowboys but of “rugged communities.” Nineteenth-century Americans ran their meetings by Robert’s Rules, took minutes, swore solemn oaths, drafted charters and elected officers. What looks like red tape today was then the machinery of self-government. He even put in a good word for hierarchy:

Centralization, not hierarchy, caused the demise of local dynamism.Lessons from the 19th Century, 2023-04-03

Greer came out of exactly that kind of institution. In 2011 he shut the blog down to serve two years as a Mormon missionary, preaching on the streets of Boston. The people who best understood those years, he found, were soldiers: both had lived in a world where every small decision carried great weight. That led to another observation. The hunger for transcendent meaning does not go away. Traditional ritual lets people “build your life on something greater”; when tradition fades, the hunger flows instead to the crusade, which asks you to give your life up. Why is our age so full of Manichean striving? His answer:

I do not wonder. The star of tradition fades. The allure of consecration must grow stronger.Questing for Transcendence, 2019-04-29

The other half of his mind is on China. He holds that the first step to understanding the Chinese Communist Party is to believe what it says about itself: from Mao to Xi, the toolkit of united front and struggle has never changed, only the line between “the people” and “the enemy.” In 2022 he founded a center to translate strategic documents that exist only in Chinese.

Put the two halves together and he has been asking a single question all along: where does a people’s capacity to act together come from, and how is it lost? Speaking to American technologists in 2026, he compared Leninist systems to the bus in the movie Speed: if it ever drops below fifty miles an hour, the bomb goes off, so it must keep hurtling toward some goal, which is now to lead the next technological revolution. Set his two lines of work side by side and the contrast is plain. On one side is a mobilization machine that cannot stop. On the other is a country where everyone once knew how to run a meeting, write a charter and clear a fallen tree, which has handed that craft over to management.

Glossary

  • Management: Greer’s term for the vast institutions ordinary people cannot reach and can only appeal to: the federal government, big corporations, university administrations, and the crowd on social media.
  • Moral culture: the sociologists Campbell and Manning’s term for a society’s default way of handling conflict: settle it yourself, go to the courts, or publicize your grievance and recruit a third party.
  • United front: the Chinese Communist Party’s mix of co-optation, infiltration and propaganda aimed at groups it cannot yet openly confront.
  • Leninist party: a vanguard party that claims to know the laws of history and mobilizes all of society toward one explicit goal.

A strange thing: the population doubled, the school boards shrank

In September 2015, deep inside a long essay on three centuries of American political culture, Greer dropped a sentence that is easy to skim past:

There are fewer school boards and municipal governments now than there in the 1950s, despite the doubling of America’s population since then.Honor, Dignity, and Victimhood, 2015-09-16

A school board is as close to politics as most Americans ever get. Your neighbors elect it, it runs your children’s school, and you can walk into its meetings and speak. When a country doubles in size, you would expect this kind of doorstep government to multiply. Instead it shrank. Greer kept returning to the ratio of students to school boards as a measure: some boards now oversee the education of hundreds of thousands of students, and at that scale a citizen’s voice barely registers.

The statistic is not shocking on its own. What is striking is how much Greer pries open with it. Campus call-out culture, the shape of Black Lives Matter protests, the across-the-board failure of the COVID response, the elite admissions race, the enormous popularity of Young Adult fiction: to him these are faces of a single phenomenon. Americans are losing the ability to act together. People have not become worse. The places where that ability used to be practiced have disappeared.

Who he is

Greer spent his childhood moving around the United States, then got into “a small, tropical university that most of you have never heard of,” Brigham Young University–Hawaii, where he studied history and political science with a focus on the wars of ancient China. He started this blog in 2009. On New Year’s Day 2011 he posted a farewell: as a member of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, he was leaving to serve a two-year mission with “little to no access to newspapers, secular books, computers, or the internet.” His “About the Author” page lists the swerves since: preaching on Boston streets, teaching Homer to the children of Beijing billionaires, photographing invasion sites on Taiwan’s beaches, being “bruised blue in Cambodian fight rings.”

He did not finish his bachelor’s degree until 2015. Yet that same year he used the blog to lay out a research program on the Chinese strategic tradition for the whole English-speaking field. He now lives in Washington, runs the Center for Strategic Translation he founded in 2022, holds a non-resident fellowship in the Foreign Policy Research Institute’s Asia Program, and is writing Folly, a history of the American people in the 21st century. The blog is still going, though since 2022 only a handful of posts appear each year.

What he wrestles with

The early blog does not sound much like the later Greer. In a January 2010 post titled “America’s Greatest Challenge — and Danger,” he declared that the republic was no longer governed by the people but by “an oligarchy of well meaning elites,” and that the people had lost all interest in governing themselves:

The United States is a nation of sheep being led to the slaughter.America’s Greatest Challenge — and Danger, 2010-01-16

That is a young man’s anger, and the conclusion jumps too far. But the question he would not put down for the next fifteen years is already there: what have the elites and the people each become, and why does a people lose the capacity to govern itself?

That August he wrote a long set of notes, “The Growth Revolution,” that laid the foundation for the question. The more classical history he read, the more he distrusted easy comparisons between ancient and modern, because around 1820 population, wealth and energy use all began to grow exponentially at once. He did the sum: between 1000 and 1500 AD, Western Europe, the fastest-growing region of the world, gained a total of $327 in per capita GDP; between 2007 and 2008 alone, American per capita GDP grew by $2,380.

500 years of growth on the part of the wealthiest static societies of the old order is equal to less than 7% of a single year’s growth on the part of their modern equivalent!Notes on the Dynamics of Human Civilization: The Growth Revolution, Part I, 2010-08-04

From this he drew a principle. People assume ideas make societies, but social structure also makes ideas. Every thinker from Plato to Hobbes assumed the masses were inconsequential, not out of prejudice, but because in a static society they really were. The growth revolution gave the masses power, and that is why democracy, socialism and nationalism all found political expression in the 1800s. As he put it: “It is said that ideas can change the world. Fewer say the obvious corollary: the world can change ideas.”

Every topic he has taken up since follows the same rule: first look at how social structure distributes power, then at what people believe. The mission years would add another dimension: people need meaning as well as power.

His ideas, told as how he found them

1. From “we’ll handle it” to “get management on our side”

It began with someone else’s paper. In September 2015 Jonathan Haidt quoted at length, on his blog, from the sociologists Bradley Campbell and Jason Manning’s “Microaggression and Moral Cultures.” The paper argued that America was developing a third moral culture. In an honor culture, status rests on others’ opinion, insults must be answered with violence, and failing to retaliate is itself a moral failing. In a dignity culture, worth is inherent and cannot be taken away; you are supposed to have thick skin, and when you must go to the courts you do so “as quickly, quietly, and rarely as possible.” In a victimhood culture, people are highly sensitive to slights and lean heavily on third parties, and the victim’s moral status rises to new heights.

The paper itself was paywalled, and Greer noted honestly that every excerpt he used came via Haidt. His own contribution came in two places.

The first was a correction to how honor culture ends. The usual story is that the state grew strong and law replaced the blood feud. Greer calls that story “wrong–or perhaps more charitably, incomplete.” He took a counterexample from the Chinese history he knew well: Warring States thinkers such as Shang Yang and Han Fei saw that private vendettas fought for reputation undermined the state, and dynasty after dynasty tried to stamp them out and failed; the avenging orphan of Zhao and the outlaws of the marsh stayed stock heroes of the stage. Roman law was mighty, yet Roman politics and warfare ran on honor. What really ended honor culture was the decline of the clan. Honor is always a corporate possession, and without an extended family ready to avenge you, it cannot be maintained.

The second was his explanation of why victimhood culture rose. He set Montgomery in 1956 next to Ferguson in 2015. The watchword of the civil rights movement was dignity. King said “it is more honorable to walk in dignity than ride in humiliation,” and one of the movement’s goals was to convince Black Americans that if they acted with unity and purpose, their destiny was theirs to make. By 2015 the most successful campaigns pushed specific incidents into the media and let outraged public opinion press whoever supervised the offender, from the firing of someone over a single tweet to a governor taking policing away from a county police department. Hence his line that contemporary protests are petitions. Why did “awareness” replace action as the goal of so many campaigns? Because when disputes are settled by winning over powerful third parties, raising awareness is the rational strategy.

Why had those third parties become so powerful? He turned to the ledger of civil society. Americans volunteer less than in 1960, join local parties and caucuses less, go to church less, read local papers less, have fewer friends. National charities want your money, not your time. Lobbying has replaced local chapters. Local businesses have been squeezed out by conglomerates. The school boards have shrunk. He wrote:

America has been atomized; her citizens live alone, connected but weakly one to another. Arrayed against each is a set of vast, impersonal bureaucracies that cannot be controlled, only appealed to.Honor, Dignity, and Victimhood, 2015-09-16

In such a structure, the most effective move is to be the unambiguous victim. He quoted Tocqueville: in times of equality each man is “both independent and weak,” and so naturally turns his gaze toward the huge authority rising above the general impotence. Then he translated the three moral cultures into three political identities:

The first is the culture of the clansman. The second is the culture of the citizen. The third is the culture of the isolated individual–or as Tocqueville would call him, the subject.Honor, Dignity, and Victimhood, 2015-09-16

In 2015 this was still an explanation of the campus. With COVID in 2020 it became an explanation of the nation. Marc Andreessen published “It’s Time to Build,” and many replied that America cannot build because of entrenched interests, regulation and perverse incentives. Greer normally prefers that kind of explanation; his “baseline assumption,” he says, is that “when intelligent people produce failure, a nest of entrenched interests and perverse incentives lie at the problem’s heart.” But this time the failure was too broad: the White House, federal agencies, states, cities, business, the media, the big civic associations, none did well. Each could be explained away on its own, but as you zoomed out to the national view, the micro-explanations stopped convincing.

His control group was 1918, when state after state and city after city improvised emergency committees in a matter of days. Their effectiveness varied, but the point is that Americans’ first reflex in a crisis was to organize locally. They were less than a generation removed from the frontier and had built school boards and county governments with their own hands; the Red Cross, the Boy Scouts, the PTA and the ACLU were all founded by that generation. “To a large extent,” he wrote, “we wander in the ruins of the world this generation built.”

Then comes his most interesting analogy:

To consistently create brilliant poets, you need a society awash in mediocre, even tawdry poetry.On Cultures That Build, 2020-06-18

Neighbors who band together to clear a fallen tree will not raise the Empire State Building. But the instinct to solve problems through “the free action of the combined powers of individuals” is a habit, practiced across countless small occasions. Today’s children rarely leave the sight of adult authority and learn to resolve conflict by appeal to it; the elite spend their youth winning over admissions committees. So he rewrote Tocqueville’s description of the passive European subject with one word changed: he thinks that everything belongs to a powerful stranger called management. His diagnosis:

A culture that does not build is a culture habituated to living under management.On Cultures That Build, 2020-06-18

In 2023 he gathered this line of work into a research program with an essay for Palladium, “A School of Strength and Character,” and a summary post on the blog. He corrected a common misreading: old America was not a nation of cowboys but of “rugged communities.” He named three virtues. First, public brotherhood, sustained by solemn oaths and ritual. Second, formality: nineteenth-century Americans found that drafting charters, electing officers, holding meetings by Robert’s Rules and taking minutes were the engines of self-rule. Third, functional hierarchy: national organizations built on lodges and chapters gave local leaders wide latitude, so wielding authority at the bottom closely mirrored wielding it at the top. He put it more sharply still:

Bureaucracy is a powerful tool for people who come from a culture of agency. But bureaucratic management slowly but surely strangles the culture that tames it, and later generations ascend to power without ever having learned how to effectively wield it.Lessons from the 19th Century, 2023-04-03

2. Tradition is smarter than you, but it cannot keep up

The second line starts with a plant. In 2018 he was reading the anthropologist Joseph Henrich’s The Secret of Our Success. The Tukanoans of the Colombian Amazon eat bitter manioc, and processing it takes days: scraping, grating, washing, separating fiber from starch, then two more days of waiting before baking. Women spend about a quarter of their day on it. A mother who decided the point was just to remove the bitterness could drop steps and still get rid of the taste, but years later her family would develop chronic cyanide poisoning. When the Portuguese carried manioc to West Africa in the seventeenth century, they did not carry the processing techniques with it, and centuries later chronic cyanide poisoning remained a serious health problem there.

He joined Henrich to the political anthropologist James C. Scott. Scott’s Seeing Like a State argues that to tax and govern, a state must make society “legible”: uniform weights and measures, national languages, ID numbers, surveyed plots. But much of what makes a society work is tacit knowledge, messy and purposeless-looking from outside, which is exactly what Henrich’s cultural evolution produces. To Greer, the social engineer who sweeps it away by rational calculation is the same figure as the European explorer starving in a land of plenty:

The only difference between the rationalist explorer and the rationalist social engineer is that the engineer has the power to force the land to starve with him.Tradition is Smarter Than You Are, 2018-08-27

A conservative reading this far will think the conclusion is obvious: leave Chesterton’s fence standing. In his last paragraphs, Greer cuts the ground from under it. In the demographic transition model, a society moves from high birth and death rates to low ones, and once the transition starts, each stage lasts only two or three generations, with family structure, mate selection and subsistence all changing. What traditions could grandparents give a child who will spend her teenage years in a different stage altogether? By the time any new tradition arises, the conditions that made it adaptive are gone.

The traditions are gone; custom is dying. In the search for happiness, rationalism is the only tool we have left.Tradition is Smarter Than You Are, 2018-08-27

This is the same judgment as the 2010 “Growth Revolution”: in the exponential age the rules have changed, and the old world’s experience cannot simply be carried over.

Eight months later he came at the same problem from the other side, this time through his own life. “Questing for Transcendence” (2019) is his most personal essay. More interesting experiences were packed into his mission years, he wrote, than into the rest of his résumé combined. Later he found that the people who best understood them were soldiers. He recalled a Facebook comment by a former Marine mechanic who had served in Afghanistan (he hadn’t saved it and gave only a paraphrase): back home and working in sales, the man found everything selfish; in Afghanistan everything he did had a purpose, here it was all meaningless. Greer recognized the feeling, and so do many returned missionaries. A soldier’s small decisions bear on his comrades’ lives; a missionary’s bear on eternity.

Humanity is both at its most heroic and its most horrifying when questing for transcendence.Questing for Transcendence, 2019-04-29

But no society of perpetual crusaders can last. Consecration is exhausting, and the next generation grows sick of grand causes and wants to get back to settled life. So how does a society meet the yearning for transcendence without burning its people’s abundance on an everlasting crusade? He found a second path in an essay by Will Wilkinson on American country music. Country songs return again and again to life’s stations: falling in love, the wedding day, the birth of a child, being cared for by your parents and then caring for them. That is meaning found by linking your life to traditions that reach far beyond you, and Greer calls it “the eternal round.” He borrows Chesterton for what it achieves: to be “astonished at the world and yet at home in it.” He puts the difference between the two paths cleanly:

The cause asks you to give up your life for something greater; the round asks you to build your life on something greater.Questing for Transcendence, 2019-04-29

Then he notices that country music was already changing when Wilkinson wrote in 2011. Fewer songs about dads changing diapers or seeing God in a child’s birth; the charts now belong to trucks, beer and girls in tight jeans. The music follows its listeners, and the listeners have left the eternal round. Rituals are not designed, so they are easier to tear down than to build, and the hunger remains. The essay ends on the line quoted in the essentials: the star of tradition fades, the allure of consecration must grow stronger. To the objection that “meaning” is too vague to measure, his reply is: “I am more concerned with those who cannot find meaning than with those who cannot measure it.”

3. Cultures are not persuaded, they are replaced

The third line answers a technical question: how does culture actually change? In January 2020 he started from a question on Twitter: which public thinker did you idolize ten or fifteen years ago but have little respect for today? Many replied “all of them.” Greer is not that harsh, but he concluded that public intellectuals have a shelf life of about seven years. He dissected Thomas Friedman’s career: war reporting in Beirut in his twenties, a peak at 52 with The World Is Flat, and by around 60 the butt of a thousand jokes.

He offered two explanations. The psychological one: fluid intelligence begins to decline in one’s thirties, and most people do their most original work between their late twenties and early forties. The sociological one is more interesting: once famous, you stop doing what gave you insight in the first place.

In each case the intellectual in question is years removed from not just the insights that delivered fame, but the activities that delivered insight.Public Intellectuals Have Short Shelf Lives—But Why?, 2020-01-29

The Friedman of the twentieth century rushed to the scene of war massacres; the Friedman of the twenty-first hurries to conference panels. Publishers will pay a hefty advance for two hundred pages of boilerplate but not for two or three years in the archives, even though that is the only way to refill the well. His advice to intellectuals is blunt: teens and twenties for gaining skills and finding the problems, fifties and sixties for teaching, managing and dispensing wisdom, and “your thirties are for solving them.” Anyone who wants to stay a thinker after forty must walk down from the summit and start again on a different mountain; he names Francis Fukuyama as an example.

A citation buried in that essay grew, eighteen months later, into the frame of another. In 2016 the sociologists Stephen Vaisey and Omar Lizardo tested four dozen attitudes in the General Social Survey and found that most value-laden attitudes are set early in life and maintained thereafter. In “Culture Wars are Long Wars” (July 2021) he applied this to American politics. Conservatives, he argued, never waged a culture war; they waged “a political war over culture,” fighting for the courts, Congress and state legislatures without ever seriously trying to change the culture, because they assumed they had the majority. The left fought a real culture war, and won the way Hemingway’s character went bankrupt: “Gradually, then suddenly.”

Cultures do not change when people replace old ideas with new ones; cultures change when people with new ideas replace the people with old ones.Culture Wars are Long Wars, 2021-07-03

America is becoming godless not because believers were argued out of their faith, but because their children and grandchildren never took it up. He holds that every great American cultural shift has taken two or three generations, a 35–50 year process. Hayek is his favorite example. In 1949, when Hayek wrote “The Intellectuals and Socialism,” the Depression seemed to have discredited laissez-faire and command economies were everywhere; three decades later came Thatcher and Reagan. Hayek’s strategy was to win over the “secondhand dealers in ideas,” not his own contemporaries. Greer compresses the logic into one line:

Culture wars are fought for the hearts of the unborn.Culture Wars are Long Wars, 2021-07-03

That is why change always looks sudden. New ideas ferment among the young while nobody in the older cohorts’ circles is converted, until a crisis arrives that the old orthodoxy cannot explain, or the numbers simply tip, and gradually becomes suddenly. He applies the same mechanism back to the public intellectual: a person’s formative ideas were built to explain the world of his youth, and when the era passes the mind struggles to keep up. Societies change by replacing generations; a single mind renews itself only by climbing a new mountain.

4. Believe what they say

The fourth line runs through China, and it too began as a correction. The American strategist Edward Luttwak had written a book on China’s strategic tradition, and in 2014 Greer wrote two long posts on the eighty-year war between the Han dynasty and the Xiongnu to show that Luttwak had misread it. By 2015 he saw that the problem was bigger than one author: every month some new book propped up a theory of “what makes China tick” with bad Chinese history. The reason was simple: English speakers could not get at the sources. He drew up a research program, and one detail tells the story:

The last complete translation of the Zuo Zhuan was published in 1872The Chinese Strategic Tradition: A Research Program (I), 2015-05-26

(A new complete translation, by Durrant, Li and Schaberg, appeared the following year.) The Book of Han, the Book of the Later Han and the Comprehensive Mirror existed only in partial translations, and the Records of the Three Kingdoms had not been translated at all. Yet these were mandatory reading for imperial civil service exams for centuries and the shared stock of precedent for every Chinese debate on war and diplomacy. He added an observation Western readers may find uncomfortable: the West reads only Sunzi, but the men who unified the Warring States and built the first great empires followed the school that attacked him.

To put matters crudely: the men who conquered and unified the world the Sunzi was written for did so by following methods that were directly opposed to its precepts.The Chinese Strategic Tradition: A Research Program (I), 2015-05-26

He later turned the same method on the Communist Party: do not guess what it thinks; read what it writes. In 2019 he took up the journalist John Garnaut’s speech “Engineers of the Soul,” on how Mao treated Stalin’s Short Course on the History of the Bolsheviks as an “encyclopaedia of Marxism.” Greer conceded that equating Xi with Stalin seems absurd at first glance, since their death tolls differ by orders of magnitude, but argued that focusing on the differences obscures as much as it reveals. Mao said in 1957 that the content of “the people” varies from period to period, so a group could be part of “the people,” then a united front target, then an enemy. From the Yan’an Rectification to today, the united front and struggle campaigns have been turned on one group after another:

The tools have never changed. The only thing that has changed is the Party’s assessment of who is an “enemy” and who is part of the “people.”Reflections on China’s Stalinist Heritage I: A Tyrant’s Toolkit, 2019-01-17

In his 2020 series “The World That China Wants,” he championed Dan Tobin’s congressional testimony, whose footnotes take up 20 of its 35 pages and which reads Xi’s 19th Party Congress report against sixty years of speeches by Mao, Deng, Jiang and Hu. Its conclusion: the Party never aimed merely to catch up, but to pass.

By 2026 this line had joined the first. Speaking to American technologists, he said the most salient difference between the two systems is that China’s has a telos: the party-state is fundamentally a set of goal-oriented institutions. Hence the Speed analogy:

Either it hurtles towards some clearly defined goal or things start to fall apart.China and the Future of Science, 2026-03-21

Under early Mao the goal was seizing power, under late Mao class struggle, from the 1980s through the 2010s getting rich, and now leading “the next round of techno scientific revolution and industrial transformation.” He cited the rankings: count high-impact papers in 2005 and six of the world’s ten most productive universities were American; today only Harvard remains, in third place. First is Zhejiang University, and second and fourth through ninth are Chinese too. To objections about fraud and citation cartels, he answered that his listeners had lost the thread: just as people fixate on the flaws of this month’s AI model, “History is made by the trend-line.” And even if Xi Jinping died of a stroke tomorrow, the scientists, labs and instruments would still be there.

How he thinks

Use a number as a crowbar. His best pieces often start from one small, hard figure: the count of school boards, $327 against $2,380, 104 footnotes filling 20 pages of Tobin’s testimony. In 2019 historians on Twitter savaged a Science paper that traced WEIRD psychology to medieval Catholic marriage law, saying the authors had not “bothered to read a history book.” Instead of picking a side, he counted the bibliography of the 174-page preprint: 242 sources, about 43 by historians and about 20 more by economic historians. He then pointed out that the underlying thesis came from a medievalist, Michael Mitterauer, and that the psychologists were testing an old argument with new methods.

Give each school half the credit. In a 2018 post he counts among the better things he has written, the economist Jared Rubin reduces culture to incentives, while Henrich’s collaborator Jonathan Schulz reduces institutions to psychometrics. Greer says neither is right. Second-generation immigrants carry the psychology of their parents’ homelands, which incentives cannot explain; but psychology alone does not produce parliaments, which were bargained out of kings desperate for war money. His formula: “Incentives are inflected through dispositions.”

Write down exactly where you were wrong. In January 2021 he published “Everything I Got Wrong in 2020.” It opens:

As I have argued that the mark of a good mind is a willingness to admit mistakes and to come to terms with why one might have made them, I am now forced into the uncomfortable position of trying to live up to my own ideals.Everything I Got Wrong in 2020, 2021-01-06

He went item by item. He was willing to bet that Bernie Sanders would win the nomination, and his roommate regrets not taking the bet. He expected America, like China, to snap into crisis mode once the pandemic’s nature was clear, an assumption he calls “disastrously naive.” His view of the Belt and Road almost completely reversed. A few days after the 2016 election, his post had opened the same way: “I was wrong. Were you?”

Where he might be wrong

First, “management” explains too much. From campus call-outs to COVID to YA fiction, there is little it cannot account for, and a theory that explains everything has to be watched closely for what it actually predicts. The key move in “On Cultures That Build” is that each failure has its own explanation, but there are too many of them, so the cause must be “culture.” That assigns every unexplained residual to a single name. One of his own 2020 errors belongs here: he expected America to pull together in the crisis as it had in the past, and it didn’t. That misjudgment then became the strongest evidence for the theory that America can no longer act together. A theory that first fails as a prediction and then succeeds as an explanation deserves some wariness.

Second, his structural account sometimes overrides the people it describes. In “Honor, Dignity, and Victimhood” he says Ta-Nehisi Coates’s narrative, that Black Americans cannot be masters of their fate, follows the outline once used by white supremacists. He immediately qualifies it: Coates is an intellectual, not an activist, and it is unfair to fault him for writing op-eds instead of organizing. But the comparison is very sharp, and it is easily read as equating a writer’s diagnosis of structural oppression with the oppressors’ propaganda. In the same essay, all of his quotations about the three moral cultures come from Haidt’s summary. He says so plainly, but the essay’s skeleton is built on secondhand material.

Third, his cohort model makes some hard predictions. In 2021 he wrote that “at some point in the next decade” the transition would arrive and America’s most popular party would be openly run by socialists, and that millennials “are a lost generation; they will persist in their errors to the end of their days.” These claims can only be checked around 2031, and at the time of writing the outcome is open. But they expose a tension in the model: if values set in youth, who will carry out the rebuilding of an agentic culture that “Questing for Transcendence” and “Lessons from the 19th Century” hope for?

Fourth, his reading of the Party has a cost. “Believe what they say” is a strong corrective to Western wishful thinking, but it naturally overrates how closely execution follows the documents. He ran into this himself. In 2018 he argued in Foreign Policy that the Belt and Road kept expanding because Xi lived in an echo chamber. In 2021 he almost completely reversed that judgment:

It is not his tyrannical grip on the party-state that creates strategic dysfunction, but his inability to control the massive bureaucratic system he heads.Everything I Got Wrong in 2020, 2021-01-06

The correction is honest, and it shows the method’s blind spot: documents state goals, bureaucracies carry them out. His 2026 reading of Chinese science carries the same risk.

Fifth, his vantage point. He is a Latter-day Saint, and in 2016 he said he was more comfortable with a Supreme Court that leans 5–4 to the right. His positive examples (the LDS church, the Marine Corps, country music, oaths and ritual) come from worlds he himself values; secular, urban and progressive communities rarely appear in a good light. That does not invalidate the arguments, but readers should know where the camera is standing.

A note: I searched for published criticism of his work and found no substantial, signed, sustained rebuttal; most of what turns up is citation and praise. So this section draws on the errors he recorded himself and on tensions visible in the posts I read. I do not summarize arguments I have not read.

Crossovers

  • Psychology · learned helplessness, reversed: Seligman’s 1967 dog experiments framed passivity as learned helplessness. In 2016 he and Steven Maier revised it in Psychological Review: the neuroscience suggests passivity is the default response, and what gets learned is control. That fits Greer: agency has to be practiced. The difference is that the lab trains an individual sense of control, while Greer means collective self-rule, which needs members and rules of order.
  • Buddhism · the formal acts of the Vinaya: the monastic sangha handles serious business such as ordination by a formal act (saṅghakamma): a motion is read, then proclaimed three times, and it passes if no one objects. Like Robert’s Rules, it uses form to let a community govern itself. Unlike a lodge, its aim is renunciation, not civic life.
  • Complexity science · Ostrom’s nested enterprises: among Elinor Ostrom’s design principles for long-lived self-governed commons is “nested enterprises,” small units with real authority stacked into larger systems. That is “centralization, not hierarchy.” Her rules govern pastures and fisheries; Greer cares about how rules form people.
  • Evolutionary biology · evolutionary mismatch: traits evolved for ancestral environments misfire in modern ones, like a taste for sugar. Greer describes a mismatch of tradition: each demographic stage lasts two or three generations, too short for cultural evolution to select. Genetic mismatch unfolds over millennia; this one is felt within decades.
  • Distributed systems · DNS delegation: the Domain Name System is strictly hierarchical, yet each level fully delegates its zone; the root servers never manage a company’s subdomains. Chapter-based associations work the same way: hierarchical but not centralized. Unlike people, DNS needs no training at the bottom to run the top.

Ten takeaways

  1. Tanner Greer’s core diagnosis: Americans no longer ask “how do we make that happen?” but “how do we get management to take our side?”
  2. Honor, dignity and victimhood cultures rest respectively on clans, voluntary associations, and distant bureaucracies.
  3. Honor culture ended mainly because clans dissolved, not because states grew strong; Chinese dynasties failed to suppress it for two thousand years.
  4. When disputes are settled by winning over powerful third parties, publicizing grievance is rational, and protest becomes petition.
  5. In 1918 American towns built epidemic committees within days; in 2020 they did not. Self-government is a habit practiced in small things.
  6. Nineteenth-century America was built by “rugged communities,” not lone cowboys; rules of order and chapters were tools of self-rule, and centralization killed them.
  7. Tradition is often smarter than individual reason, but each stage of the demographic transition lasts only two or three generations, too fast for tradition to adapt.
  8. The hunger for transcendence does not vanish: when ritual fades, it flows into crusades that ask people to give their lives to a cause.
  9. Cultures change by generational replacement, not persuasion, so change comes gradually, then suddenly; public intellectuals last about seven years.
  10. To understand the Chinese Communist Party, read its documents: its united front and struggle tools never changed, only who counts as “the people.”

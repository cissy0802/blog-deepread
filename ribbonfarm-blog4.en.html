Venkatesh Rao · Ribbonfarm
You Have to Flatten the World to Read It

2007–2024 · 1,117 posts · 2.1 million words · 60 authors, of whom he alone wrote 875 posts and 1.54 million words · 13,230 comments · Retired November 2024, relaunched as a static archive April 2026 · This page is based on 12 posts read in full, spanning August 2007 to October 2024

In the autumn of 2009, an aerospace engineering PhD working at a Xerox research centre published an essay arguing that the sitcom The Office was not a pile of office gags.

It is a fully realized theory of management that falsifies 83.8% of the business section of the bookstore. The Gervais Principle, 2009

The 83.8% was made up — a fake number precise to one decimal place, which is itself the joke. But the post got picked up by Slashdot, and became the most-commented essay of the blog's seventeen years (283 comments), as well as one of the posts its author would go on to cite most often himself (34 later posts link back to it).

Here is what it says. A company has three layers: Losers, the Clueless, and Sociopaths. The labels are borrowed from a Hugh MacLeod cartoon. Loser is not an insult — it is meant economically. These are people who made a bad bargain, trading long-term economic liberty for short-term stability: "traded freedom for a paycheck in short." They produce, but are not paid in proportion to what they create. So the rational move is to put in the bare minimum.

Which sets up the counterintuitive part: if you over-perform at that level you will get promoted — not because you are good, but because you have revealed that you are a fool.

The simple reason is that if you over-perform at the Loser level, it is clear that you are an idiot. The Gervais Principle, 2009

You already signed a losing contract, and now you are delivering extra, making it worse. So you get promoted into middle management and become one of the Clueless — the layer whose function is to absorb consequences on behalf of the people above.

The man who wrote this is Venkatesh Rao, usually Venkat Rao. PhD in aerospace engineering from Michigan; senior researcher at a Xerox research centre from 2006 to 2011; started Ribbonfarm in July 2007 and wrote it for seventeen years — 1,117 posts, 2.1 million words — until it stopped in November 2024.

Underneath all those words there is one thing, and it was already there in his very first post. That post is about sitting on the second floor of a coffee shop in Ithaca and watching a man on the street below with an enormous white parrot. He spends the whole essay watching not the parrot but the passers-by — the adults working to file this parrot into an existing category, the children not needing to file it at all.

a free mind is one which the parrot can occupy easily, and stay in as long as it chooses. The Parrot, 2007

That is the thing: you have to compress the world into a version you can read before you can act inside it, and the act of compressing is exactly what you then cannot see. The next seventeen years are that one mechanism, redrawn across different subject matter.

The post he links back to more than any other — cited by 50 later posts out of 1,117, well ahead of the runner-up at 34 — is about how the Prussians killed their own forests. The state could not read a natural forest: mixed species, growing at random, impossible to count or tax. So it invented "scientific forestry": clear everything, plant one high-yield species in neat rows. The first generation grew beautifully. The second generation died. All the messy things that had been cleared away — fungi, undergrowth, insects, leaf litter — turned out to be the reason the forest had been alive.

The case comes from the anthropologist James C. Scott's Seeing Like a State, and Rao says so plainly and repeatedly. What he added is the line that turned a piece of political history into an everyday instrument:

though this failure mode is easiest to describe in terms of high-modernist ideology, it is actually a basic failure mode for human thought that is time and ideology neutral. A Big Little Idea Called Legibility, 2010

In other words: this is not a Prussian problem, or a Soviet one. It is the problem with how you restructure your own résumé, and with how a product manager draws a user persona. And the thing driving it is not arrogance but anxiety — "legibility quells the anxieties evoked by apparent chaos." You do not simplify because you want control. You simplify because looking at a mess is unpleasant.

Two years later he turned the same mechanism over. This time the compression is not imposed from above by power; you are doing it yourself, and it feels good. He called it the Manufactured Normalcy Field:

Life as we live it has this familiar sense of being a static, continuous present. Our ongoing time travel (at a velocity of one second per second) never seems to take us to a foreign place. It is always 4 PM; it is always tea-time. Welcome to the Future Nauseous, 2012

Here he had an advantage nobody else writing about this had. Climb rates, bank angles, acceleration profiles — those airliner parameters were his homework problems in graduate school. He knew the limits were not physical ceilings but design choices, held there so that you would not notice you were flying.

Airline passengers don't fly. Welcome to the Future Nauseous, 2012

You are sitting in an aluminium tube at roughly two-thirds the speed of sound, and that tube is in fact capable of some fairly alarming acrobatics. You cannot feel any of it, because the whole system is engineered so you cannot. Technology does not spread by changing experience. It spreads by refusing to. The phone descended from the telephone, not the calculator; the web arrived as "documents" and "pages." Each new thing has to disguise itself as an old thing to get through the door.

And the ending is not "so wake up." He says that when change outruns the Field's ability to stretch, you will not be knocked down:

You're not going to be knocked out cold. You're just going to throw up in some existential sense of the word. Welcome to the Future Nauseous, 2012

The blog ran to its last post in November 2024. And the harshest verdict anyone has passed on those seventeen years was written by him, not by any critic:

This blog was sponsored by ZIRP. Ribbonfarm is Retiring, 2024

He does not mean it as a joke. Cheap servers, open-source software, distribution that cost nothing, a large supply of clever people with time on their hands — the entire set of conditions that let one person write for seventeen years without charging for it was a product of the zero-interest-rate era. He does not expect to see rates like that again in his lifetime. Which means he does not expect to see a blogosphere like that again either. A man who spent seventeen years arguing that people cannot see which simplification they are living inside finished by identifying the one he had been living inside himself.

For the "premium mediocre" list and the call option underneath it, his definition of a consumer as "a novel and stable pattern of behavior," his three contrarian writing rules (one of which is to cite yourself ten times as often as you cite anyone else), and the passage where Scott Alexander dismantles the Gervais Principle using Green, Red and Blue people — switch to Full above.

Glossary

  • Legibility: the key word on this page. How readable a complex thing is to an outside power — whether it can be counted, registered, taxed, administered. Making something legible usually means first reshaping it into something tidier.
  • Seeing Like a State: the 1998 book by the anthropologist and political scientist James C. Scott, and the source of this use of "legibility." Rao's famous essay is an extended retelling of it, which he says clearly himself.
  • High modernism: the early-twentieth-century belief that society could be redesigned wholesale by scientific reason. Purpose-built capitals like Brasília are its monuments.
  • The MacLeod company hierarchy: a cartoon by Hugh MacLeod dividing a company into three tiers — Losers at the bottom, the Clueless in the middle, Sociopaths on top. Rao borrowed the labels and admits their literal meanings do not quite match what he is pointing at.
  • The Office: the sitcom created by Ricky Gervais (Rao mostly analyses the American version). The Gervais Principle is named after him.
  • Slashdotted: Slashdot was the major geek news site of the 2000s. Reaching its front page brought enormous traffic, and usually took a small blog's server down with it.
  • ZIRP: zero interest-rate policy. After the 2008 financial crisis central banks held rates near zero for years, making capital extremely cheap — the economic backdrop to a decade of free internet writing.
  • Internal backlink centrality: how many times a post is cited by later posts on the same site. The figures used on this page come from the statistics page the Ribbonfarm archive publishes itself, not from my own estimate.

One Strange Thing: A Fake Number Precise to One Decimal Place

On 7 October 2009, an engineer working at a Xerox research centre outside Rochester, New York, published an analysis of a sitcom. The opening settles the matter immediately:

The Office is not a random series of cynical gags aimed at momentarily alleviating the existential despair of low-level grunts. It is a fully realized theory of management that falsifies 83.8% of the business section of the bookstore. The Gervais Principle, 2009

The 83.8% has no source, because he made it up. A number precise to one decimal place, of unknown provenance, delivered with total confidence — this move recurs endlessly on this blog: a form that pretends to rigour, carrying a judgement he actually means. The number is fake. The claim that most of the business section is wrong is sincere.

Then Slashdot put it on the front page. In his own retrospective phrasing, this post "put ribbonfarm on the map." Fifteen years later the archive's own statistics show it is the most-commented post on the site (283), the most externally linked (1,272 points on Hacker News), and the second most cited by his own later work (34 times).

The part worth pausing on: this is an aerospace engineering PhD, employed in the research arm of a photocopier company, who in his spare time produced a self-contained theory of organisations by taking apart a television comedy. No business school background, no experience as a manager, no plan to write a book. That combination is the shape of everything he wrote afterwards.

Who He Is

Venkatesh Rao, usually Venkat Rao. BS in mechanical engineering from IIT Bombay (1997), MS in systems and control (1999) and PhD in aerospace engineering (2003) from the University of Michigan, postdoctoral researcher at Cornell (2004–2006, working on command and control systems), then senior researcher at the Xerox Research Center Webster from 2006 to 2011. He left Xerox in 2011 and has been an independent consultant and writer since.

He started the blog in July 2007. The name comes from the ribbon farms of eighteenth-century Detroit — long narrow strips of land cut back from the river so each holding got a sliver of waterfront. His gloss on it describes a writer's position online exactly: a blog is something trying to get a thin slice of attention from the great river of eyeballs that is the web.

One detail says a great deal about him. For the first twelve years the blog's tagline was "Experiments in refactored perception" — which he annotates as a geek joke: changing how you see the world by trying to rewire the software inside your head through writing. In 2019 he changed it to "Constructions in magical thinking," and described that mode as "disciplined solipsism and escapism." A man willing to file his own late period under "escapism" is not being gentle with himself.

In January 2015 Sarah Perry became a contributing editor and the blog turned from a solo project into a group one — 60 authors eventually, though he alone wrote 875 posts and 1.54 million words, close to three-quarters of the site. From 2012 to 2019 he ran an annual unconference called Refactor Camp.

On the dormancy: Ribbonfarm formally retired in November 2024, with the last post on 13 November, his fiftieth birthday. In April 2026 the whole site became a static archive; the domain and all links are preserved, and every link cited on this page was tested and opens. He continues to write elsewhere, on Substack.

What He Was Wrestling With

Pull three Ribbonfarm posts at random and you get: one on the financial history of the East India Company, one on the career of a hand model, one on why teenagers in the Pacific Northwest build forts in the living room. No visible through-line.

But the through-line is in the first post, and the first post is about a parrot.

In August 2007 he is sitting at a window table on the second floor of a coffee shop on the Ithaca Commons, looking down, and there is a man with an enormous white parrot. The owner walks off; the parrot stays. He does not write about the parrot. He writes about the passers-by — about what each person's face has to do in the instant it registers a parrot. One line is both cruel and exact: your face must look like the face of a person who has seen a parrot. The adults are busy filing it. The children do not have to file anything; they are simply looking at a parrot.

The parrot is rare in adult life. For the child, everything is a parrot. The Parrot, 2007

The parrot is the thing that has not yet been filed. And adult life runs by filing everything within half a second — filing is what lets you act, and filing is also what means you are not looking.

He never put this down. Stated plainly, his root question is:

People have to compress a complex reality into a readable version before they can operate inside it. So: who made that compression, for whose convenience, what got discarded, and who pays for what was discarded?

Several things about that framing are unusual and worth making explicit.

First, he does not think simplification is avoidable. This is not a series of essays saying the world is complicated, so stop simplifying it. The parrot post already concedes that you cannot go on not filing. What he wants to know is the shape of the simplification: who performed it, and where the cost lands.

Second, he puts power and comfort inside the same machine. The Prussian state simplifying a forest is violence from above; you simplifying flight into "sitting in a chair watching a film" is voluntary, comfortable and free. He insists these are two faces of one mechanism — a genuinely uncommon position in this subject area, where most writers slide halfway through into the critique-of-power lane and stay there.

Third, and this is where he is most often misread: he is not telling you to take the glasses off. His repeated point is that you cannot, and that if you did you would be sick. All he can do is make you aware the glasses are there, and then swap a lens deliberately at a chosen moment. That is the literal meaning of "experiments in refactored perception."

His Ideas, Told as How He Found Them

1 · Making It Readable Is What Makes It Brittle

Of 1,117 posts, the one his own later work cites most is "A Big Little Idea Called Legibility" from July 2010 — 50 backlinks, nearly half again as many as the runner-up. It is the load-bearing wall of the whole blog.

And it does not open like a theory essay. It opens with photographs of forests.

On the left, a natural forest: mixed species, uneven heights, deadfall, undergrowth, vines, rotting leaves. On the right, a stretch of forest the Prussians remade in the late eighteenth century: one species, evenly spaced, straight, in rows.

Why change it? Because a natural forest cannot be counted. What the state wants is a number that fits in a table: how many cubic metres of usable timber per year, and therefore how much tax. To a surveyor's eye a wild forest is close to unreadable. Hence "scientific forestry" — clear it, plant only the highest-yielding species, in a grid.

The first generation grew superbly. The yield figures were excellent and the model spread across Europe.

The second generation collapsed. The Germans eventually coined a word for it: Waldsterben, forest death. All the mess that had been cleared out — fungal networks, understory shrubs, insects, the humus layer, mixed ages — turned out not to be noise but the reason the forest was alive. The alteration made to render the forest readable had made it brittle.

Rao got the case from James C. Scott's Seeing Like a State — he says throughout that it is Scott's book, and even that his own post's main function is retelling — and then did two things Scott did not.

The first was to compress it into a portable recipe. He renders the pattern from Scott's long book as seven deliberately unkind steps:

  1. Look at a complex and confusing reality, such as the social dynamics of an old city
  2. Fail to understand all the subtleties of how the complex reality works
  3. Attribute that failure to the irrationality of what you are looking at, rather than your own limitations
  4. Come up with an idealized blank-slate vision of what that reality ought to look like
  5. Argue that the relative simplicity and platonic orderliness of the vision represents rationality
  6. Use authoritarian power to impose that vision, by demolishing the old reality if necessary
  7. Watch your rational Utopia fail horribly

Laying the seven steps out like that lets you recognise the pattern in your own conference room. And the mechanism sentence is the core of the piece:

This imposed simplification, in service of legibility to the state's eye, makes the rich reality brittle, and failure follows. A Big Little Idea Called Legibility, 2010

The second thing matters more, and is why the post became load-bearing: he carried it out of political history.

Scott's book is about states. Rao says this failure mode does not belong to states, or to any ideology:

though this failure mode is easiest to describe in terms of high-modernist ideology, it is actually a basic failure mode for human thought that is time and ideology neutral. A Big Little Idea Called Legibility, 2010

He immediately turns the knife on the most popular "moderate and reasonable" alternatives of the day (and of ours): behavioural economics and "choice architecture," and the design maxim about paving the cowpaths. These grant no immunity, he says — paving the cowpaths naively is itself an instance of the failure mode. Because the question was never whether you consulted reality. It is what shape you intend to press reality into, and for whose convenience.

Then comes the sentence that turns the whole thing from political science into psychology. What tempts us in, he says, is that

legibility quells the anxieties evoked by apparent chaos. A Big Little Idea Called Legibility, 2010

You do not simplify because you want control. You simplify because looking at a mess is unpleasant. That line converts an essay about state violence into something usable this afternoon — because the mess making you uncomfortable is probably sitting in your own to-do list.

2 · Organisations Do Not Get Sick; They Are the Sickness

Back to 2009, which deserves to be told properly, because it is both the most famous thing on the blog and the most easily read too shallowly.

His starting point is not a theory but a discomfort about an observation: a great deal of what happens in The Office cannot be explained by "these people are stupid." Under the stupidity assumption the characters' behaviour is incoherent. Under the assumption that everyone is acting rationally by the rules of their own layer, all of it coheres.

He then notices that management bestsellers assume an organisation has a healthy state, with problems as lesions upon it. He inverts the assumption entirely:

organizations don't suffer pathologies; they are intrinsically pathological constructs. Idealized organizations are not perfect. They are perfectly pathological. The Gervais Principle, 2009

That is not an epigram but a premise you can run deductions from. If the pathology is the structure, you ought to be able to derive all the strange phenomena from the structure. That is what he does.

The word "Loser" has to be pinned down first, because nearly every misreading starts there. He is emphatic that this is economic, not a judgement of character:

They've given up some potential for long-term economic liberty (as capitalists) for short-term economic stability. Traded freedom for a paycheck in short. They actually produce, but are not compensated in proportion to the value they create […] The Gervais Principle, 2009

Pin that down and the famous counterintuitive line becomes a pure deduction rather than a pose: if you have already signed a losing contract, then working harder makes the contract worse. And that fact is observable:

The simple reason is that if you over-perform at the Loser level, it is clear that you are an idiot. The Gervais Principle, 2009

Promotion now means something new. You are not promoted for being good; you are promoted for revealing that you will not negotiate on your own behalf — promoted into the interlayer that exists to absorb consequences. Whereas the people who put in the minimum from the start, knowing exactly what they are doing, are the ones groomed upward. Both dotted lines in the diagram bypass the middle because performing normally reveals neither that you are a fool nor that you have seen through the game, and so carries no information.

The real destructive power of the model is that it reinterprets the entire language of a company: the three layers are speaking three different kinds of speech, and middle-management meeting talk sounds like idling because it was never meant to transmit information. It is also why he lands the essay on the rationality of the Losers. The coldest line in it is for them:

They mortgage their lives away, and hope to die before their money runs out. The Gervais Principle, 2009

Two years later he turned the same knife on the consumption side, in "The Gollum Effect." He begins by revisiting a definition he had derived years earlier from Druckerian first principles — "a customer isn't a human being. A customer is a novel and stable pattern of behavior" — and then says that every successful post he built on it made him more uneasy about what it implied. Because if you add those behaviour patterns up, what you get is a hollowed-out person:

consumerism is not about humans consuming products. It is about products consuming humans. The Gollum Effect, 2011

Gollum is the character in The Lord of the Rings emptied out by the ring. The cleverness of the ring is that, unlike a drug, it does not kill its host — it keeps the host functional, employable, solvent. This dovetails exactly with the organisational argument: on the production side a person is compressed into a role; on the consumption side, into a set of purchases. Both keep only the part that is needed.

3 · The Normalcy Field: Technology Spreads by Refusing to Change Experience

In May 2012 he wrote what I think is his most beautiful piece.

It starts from something everyone notices and nobody takes seriously: the future arrives, and never feels like the future. The phone in your pocket outperforms Captain Kirk's communicator and you do not feel you are living in the future.

There are two much-quoted lines about this: McLuhan's "we look at the present through a rear-view mirror; we march backwards into the future," and William Gibson's "the future is already here; it is just unevenly distributed." Both, he says, have serious limitations, and what they jointly miss needs a name. His name for it is the Manufactured Normalcy Field:

Life as we live it has this familiar sense of being a static, continuous present. Our ongoing time travel (at a velocity of one second per second) never seems to take us to a foreign place. It is always 4 PM; it is always tea-time. Welcome to the Future Nauseous, 2012

Then comes the lever, and it is a lever nobody else could reach for.

He turns to the modern airliner: climb rates, bank angles and acceleration profiles, all held within strict limits. And then he adds, almost in passing — back in the day, I used to do homework problems to calculate these limits. An aerospace PhD sitting in a passenger cabin knows those numbers are not physical ceilings but design decisions, held there so that you will not notice you are flying.

Airline passengers don't fly. Welcome to the Future Nauseous, 2012

You are travelling at roughly two-thirds the speed of sound inside an aluminium tube capable of genuinely frightening acrobatics. The experience has been engineered into "a departure lounge that shakes a bit." You are not experiencing flight. You are travelling inside a purpose-built field of normalcy.

From there he makes the generalisation that matters. If flight entered daily life that way, presumably all technology does: successful products are precisely those that do not attempt to move user experiences significantly, even when the underlying technology has shifted radically.

What makes this counterintuitive is that it negates the industry's favourite story about itself. The standard account says new technology wins by being better. He says new technology wins by successfully disguising itself as an old thing. The phone's ancestor is the telephone, not the calculator; the web's ancestors are "documents" and "pages," though underneath it is neither. Every generation of new technology needs an old metaphor for a visa. And once admitted, that metaphor becomes the boundary of what you can imagine — which is why genuinely new things are first built in the shape of old ones, and why it takes years before anyone notices they could be something else.

He also corrects Gibson in passing. What is unevenly distributed, he says, is not the future but the Field: a small number of people (pilots, engineers) stand outside it, holding an instrumental understanding of the technology, and for exactly that reason often lacking an appreciative one.

Then the ending. The futurists' warning about Future Shock — change so fast it knocks people down — has the symptom wrong. What actually happens is that the Field gets stretched to its limit: you can still operate everything, but you can no longer register where you are. He gives that state a thoroughly undignified and therefore very accurate name, Future Nausea:

You're not going to be knocked out cold. You're just going to throw up in some existential sense of the word. Welcome to the Future Nauseous, 2012

He offers no remedy. He says only that he would like to prepare, and that he wishes some science fiction writers would write a few nauseating stories. This is characteristic: name a state you previously had no name for, and then stop there.

Three years later, in "On the Design of Escaped Realities," he added an elegant complement. Everyone was talking about VR headsets at the time. He said they had the source of strength backwards:

What determines the strength of an escaped reality is not the sophistication of the technology enabling it, but the number of other people who believe absolutely in it. On the Design of Escaped Realities, 2015

So a thousand-year-old stone temple — with a few hundred people believing in it absolutely and organising their entire lives around it with stone tools and spears — is a far more powerful virtual reality than a headset. The Field was never built by technology. It was built by how many people are in it.

4 · Premium Mediocre: When the Mark Knows

The August 2017 essay is this machine's late-stage form, and his most widely circulated coinage.

The discovery was small: eating at a chain called Veggie Grill, he noticed the food had a quality he could not name — not good, not exactly bad, but mediocrity wearing a premium wrapper. He coined the term, then did something he often does: he threw it to several dozen friends as a game of naming premium mediocre things, and notes honestly that most of the examples below, and all the really good ones, came from other people.

Premium mediocre is the finest bottle of wine at Olive Garden. Premium mediocre is cupcakes and froyo. Premium mediocre is "truffle" oil on anything (no actual truffles are harmed in the making of "truffle" oil), and extra-leg-room seats in Economy. […] Premium mediocre is food that Instagrams better than it tastes. The Premium Mediocre Life of Maya Millennial, 2017

The term spread on the accuracy of that list. But what makes it an idea rather than a bit is the turn that follows — and that turn is a correction of his own previous decade.

In "The Gollum Effect" the hollowed-out person does not know. In the legibility essay the simplified forest has no opinion. Here, neither holds:

Maya Millennial is aware that what she is consuming is mediocre at its core, and only "premium" in some peripheral (and importantly, cheap, such as French-for-no-reason branding) ways. But she consumes it anyway. She is aware that her consumption is tasteless, yet she pretends it is tasteful anyway. The Premium Mediocre Life of Maya Millennial, 2017

Or, in the phrase he borrows from the scholar of taste Gabe Duquette: she consumes pablum knowing it's pablum.

That lifts the whole thing out of false consciousness. She has not been fooled. She is executing a strategy.

And what the strategy is forms the sturdiest part of the essay. He calls it a naked call option: you live as the person you will be if you succeed, and push the bill to an uncertain future. In an economy where returns are wildly unequal and largely a matter of luck, announcing loudly that you think it is mostly luck is the surest way to be excluded from the lottery — so you have to perform belief.

Harsher still are the two audiences he identifies. Backward, the performance is for the previous generation: they get to be certain they set their children up, and the children preserve that certainty for them. Forward, it is for the funders: proof that the system still works, that the geese laying golden eggs should not despair at the rate of progress. An entire generation's ordinary consumption, explained as a two-way, considerate, expensive conspiracy.

Then the shortest possible footnote: So you fake it till you make it. Unless you don't.

Three years later he wrote the last link in the sequence. "The Internet of Beefs" asks what happens when someone cannot even afford that option — when identity has nothing left to hang on.

His answer: people fight, because conflict at least supplies an identity. He describes the online battlefield as a feudal structure — named "knights" trading fire at the top, and beneath them anonymous, interchangeable, volunteer "mooks" fighting under various banners, for whom the highest honour is to be noticed once by a knight they fight for. His diagnostic sentence borrows from crash-only design in computing:

A beef-only thinker is someone you cannot simply talk to. Anything that is not an expression of pure, unqualified support for whatever they are doing or saying is received as a mark of disrespect, and a provocation to conflict. The Internet of Beefs, 2020

And the root cause he gives for the whole melee is the terminus of his seventeen-year through-line: we are beefing because we no longer know who we are, each of us individually, and collectively as a species. What is left when the compression finally fails is not clarity. It is a fight.

How He Thinks

What transfers from a writer of seventeen years is rarely the conclusions. The following are induced from the essays above rather than copied from his self-descriptions — except the first, which he did write down, and which left fingerprints in the data.

1. Cite yourself ten times as often as you cite anyone else. In "The Calculus of Grit" (2011) he lays out three writing disciplines, one of which sounds outrageously self-regarding:

it sounds narcissistic, but if you're not referencing your own work at least 10 times as often as you're referencing others, you're in trouble in the intrinsic navigation world. The Calculus of Grit, 2011

What is lovely is that the discipline is verifiable in the statistics fifteen years later: the legibility post has 50 internal backlinks, Gervais 34, the normalcy field 25, the Gollum effect 22. He did not write 1,117 scattered essays; he built a structure with load-bearing members, bolting each new idea onto a few old columns. It also explains why a single post of his often reads as erratic while a batch of them suddenly clicks. His stated reason is not vanity but navigation: without your own inertia you get shoved around by external forces like a pollen grain in Brownian motion.

2. Writing hours do not count; rewriting hours do. Another of the three. His phrasing: "My aha! moment came when I realized that it isn't the writing hours that count, it is the rewriting hours. Everybody writes. People who are trying to walk the path towards mastery rewrite." This is a precise dissolution of the ten-thousand-hours idea: ten thousand hours of writing is no filter at all for anyone literate, because everyone writes every day.

3. If it feels hard, you picked wrong. Close to heresy in the current climate:

if you find that what you are doing is ridiculously hard for you, it is the wrong thing for you to be doing. The Calculus of Grit, 2011

The argument is not inspirational. What looks from outside like superhuman grit, he says, is usually someone doing a thing that costs them little — "they think it is hard for me because it would be hard for them." So the correct move is not over or through the obstacle but around it. This and the first rule are the same rule twice: drop external frames of reference, navigate on internal ones.

4. His primary instrument is coinage, not argument. The most distinctive thing about him and the one most in need of stating plainly. Legibility, the Gervais Principle, the Gollum effect, the Manufactured Normalcy Field, premium mediocre, the Internet of Beefs, domestic cozy, cozyweb, salt-seeking — his life's output is essentially a coinage production line. And the coinages share a recipe: a highly concrete, low-status image bolted to a highly abstract mechanism. Gollum is a fantasy-novel character carrying a sociology of consumption; Olive Garden is America's most-mocked chain restaurant carrying the class position of a generation. The low-status vehicle does two jobs: it makes the abstraction touchable, and it smuggles in a verdict (you know instantly this is not a good thing), which saves an entire stretch of argument.

5. Pop culture as a crowbar, not as decoration. The operational version of the above. A sitcom, a cartoon, a chain restaurant's wine list — what these have in common is that the reader already has first-hand experience of them. He does not need to make you trust a field study; you have eaten at Olive Garden. This is how he gets around the problem of not being a credentialed expert in any of his subjects: he never tries to know the field better than the specialists, he just rearranges experience the reader already owns.

6. Name it, then stop. He almost never gives advice. "Welcome to the Future Nauseous" ends with him saying he would like to prepare; "The Internet of Beefs" ends with us needing to work out who we are first. In this genre that is a deliberate choice — the moment you issue a recommendation, the recommendation becomes the next compression. His job is to make you see the glasses, not to fit you with a new pair.

Where He May Be Wrong

1. Scott Alexander took the Gervais Principle apart once, and did it well. In a 2022 review he first calls Rao a "postrationalist heresiarch" and grants that the book "feels like what psychoanalysis would be like if it weren't so devoted to making itself incomprehensible." Then he asks the question that needed asking: is any of it true?

I don't find myself or the people I know best falling clearly into any of these archetypes. They're useful to have around. I can see pieces of all of them. But none are a great match. Scott Alexander, Book Review: The Gervais Principle, 2022

His kill shot is to invent three types on the spot: Green (introverted, long hair, likes the cold, complains too much), Red (cheerful, gets mad at little things, loves pets and children), Blue (financially savvy, bad at romance, natural leader, enjoys biking). Most people, he notes, will find they have some traits of each — "but that's just a natural result of the system being made up and useless." And: "Rao said (in one sentence) that everyone has multiple types. But then what's the use of this categorization system?"

He also points out that the Principle's core claim is testable — look at a sales firm or an investment bank and correlate first-year performance with promotion — and states his own prior: he would naively expect corporate top echelons to be former normal-to-over-performers, and says that if someone found they were in fact under-performers, it would be a big update in Rao's favour. He could not find a dataset. Fifteen years on, so far as I can find, nobody has run it. This is a theory that has been widely quoted, printed as a book, and used by many people to make career decisions, and it has never been checked at the one point where it is most easily falsified.

2. The cost of coinage: a good name feels like a finished explanation. This is the bill for method 4 above. "Premium mediocre" names a pattern with great precision, but it does not explain it — the click you feel on finishing comes from being able to point at the thing, not from knowing why it happens. His terms are good enough to substitute for argument, and in his weaker essays they do. It also explains why Ribbonfarm's imitators tend to be so bad: they copied the coinage technique without the work he did before coining.

3. A systematic tilt: this lens only fits power, status and predation. Nearly all his core mechanisms — organisations, consumption, attention, online conflict — resolve into who is extracting what from whom. The lens is very sharp, at the cost of leaving almost no room for "this basically works" or "these people genuinely want to do the job well." The Gervais Principle has no slot for someone who is not a Loser, not Clueless and not a Sociopath, but simply likes their work. He treats this as realism; it may also just be the shape of the lens. There is an honest self-diagnosis buried in "The Return of the Barbarian," though he aims it at civilisations: he insists that refinement is not the same as advancement — and for a writer known above all for conceptual density, that is a ruler he should hold against himself first.

4. The harshest criticism was written by him. In the 2024 retirement notice he says the entire three-act Ribbonfarm story was a product of low interest rates:

This blog was sponsored by ZIRP. Ribbonfarm is Retiring, 2024

He adds that this is not an entirely flippant observation: the blog in its heyday was a product of open-source software, cheap hosting, and above all free distribution. He even names names, saying several of his big hits — including "Premium Mediocre" and 2013's "The Locust Economy" — had ZIRPy subtexts. A man who spent seventeen years arguing that people cannot see which simplification they inhabit finally turned the instrument on himself: what looks like timeless insight may be the product of one particular set of macroeconomic conditions. He does not expect to see such rates again in his lifetime, and therefore does not expect to see such a blogosphere again either.

He is equally unsparing about his own prose. Explaining why he never felt at home on Substack, where the prevailing mode is an earnest "chamber music" style, he says: "I'm too much of a careless shitposter, and the chamber music style calls for a certain earnestness of approach." He also discloses something many readers do not know: after 2020 he deliberately stopped writing for virality, and went out of his way to write anti-viral posts. "The Internet of Beefs" was "not just my last viral post, it was my last viral-intent post."

And one final practical limit: the blog is over. It stopped in November 2024 and became a static archive in April 2026. He writes elsewhere still, but the mind described on this page is one that has finished its shift.

Sarah Perry
Modern life lacks nothing but the burdens

Sarah Perry · blog The View from Hell, written early on under the name Sister Y · an independent writer with no academic post, author of Every Cradle Is a Grave (2014) · Dormant: her last Ribbonfarm post, dated 2019-08-15, announces that she is stepping down as contributing editor. · Corpus: The View from Hell now holds just 6 posts (2012-01 to 2015-01) under the tagline “Old stuff. Enjoy each other and don’t create new life.” Earlier posts (going back to at least 2011) have been deleted, and this environment could not reach web archives, so they were not read. The bulk of her thinking is on Ribbonfarm: 49 posts from 2015-01 to 2019-08, about 157,000 words. Posts on the Carcinisation site after 2020 are signed “birguslatro”; I could not verify that this is her, so they are left out. · This page is based on 15 posts actually read (2012–2019) plus two reviews of her book by other writers.

Most American kitchen knives are dull. Sarah Perry noticed this in 2012 and found a reason. Stainless steel doesn’t rust; leave it in the sink overnight and it will just sit there “shining up at you damply.” But it won’t take a good edge. So people stopped learning to sharpen, vegetables started coming pre-cut in plastic bags, and the cook was asked for less and less and got less and less back. A carbon steel knife is the opposite. You have to hunt for it in some cluttered restaurant-supply shop in Chinatown, and you have to dry it right away or it will start “rusting at you accusingly.” But it gets sharp, and whether you can keep it sharp is part of your worth as a cook.

Knives have become easy, non-demanding, non-functional, and sad.Fungibility and the Loss of Demandingness, 2012-11-18

That is the first piece of her mind: what a thing demands of you is exactly what it can give back. The market’s talent is removing burdens one by one. But a thing anyone can get for money can’t tell you that you have become better.

Three years later she moved that piece onto ritual. Anthropologists have long wondered why so many cultures sacrifice animals, fast, dance all night, and torment adolescents at initiation. She borrowed costly signaling from evolutionary biology: the more ridiculous the peacock’s tail, the healthier the bird must be; the more you sacrifice for a group, the more believable your loyalty. But she found that this explains only half of it. The anthropologist Richard Sosis measured how long nineteenth-century American communes survived. The more demanding religious communes lasted longer, while secular communes did badly however much they demanded. The difference was that religious ritual evokes a particular mental state, a sense that this is undeniably true. Hence her formula: cooperation is the fire, sacrifice the fuel, the ritual mental state the oxygen.

Indeed, the human order is a ritual order, not a rational one.What is Ritual?, 2015-02-11

So what is modern life missing? Her second piece comes from the developmental psychologist Philippe Rochat. Human self-consciousness is forced on us by others: we learn to see ourselves through other people’s eyes. The trouble is that the first-person self is always inflated and the third-person self always deflated, and the gap between them never closes. Mirrors, photographs, exams, social networks: modern life is full of mirrors that remind you of the gap every day. Meanwhile religion and tradition are retreating as sources of meaning, and the self has to carry the meaning of a whole life alone. Ritual used to be where you could set the self down for a while, and it disappeared just when we needed it most.

The third piece is mess. She called herself pathologically messy, and she thought about mess with unusual precision. A field of solid black is not a mess; a field of random noise is not a mess either. A page of overlapping text and logos is a mess, though it takes fewer bits to describe than the noise. Clouds are never a mess; a drywall house falls into one the moment it decays. Her answer: mess is the orders implied by things fighting each other and going unfulfilled. Nature is never a mess because each of its steps preserves the structure already there and grows a little from it. Later she carried the rule into daily life: don’t live by an ideal schedule; start from the few things that already feel good and grow outward. She called it deep laziness.

Put the three pieces together and you get the core of her: the old ways our ancestors met their material needs also quietly supplied meaning, belonging and beauty. Modern life meets material needs more efficiently, those side benefits vanish with the old ways, and individual imagination cannot replace them, because they were grown by small groups, boundaries and ritual in the first place. In her words: “Individual imagination is weak; evolution is strong.”

Her 2019 farewell post was very much her. She had given a talk with a beautiful solution: the bright yellow lichen grows in rings around the pines near Lake Tahoe because woodpeckers peck rings of holes first. After the talk she climbed more mountains and found the solution was probably wrong. “I think my almost-certainly-wrong answer is more satisfying than the truth,” she wrote. Then she mentioned that she had quit carbohydrates, which had somehow cured her mental illness, and that she had figured out how not to write.

Glossary

  • Costly signaling — An idea from evolutionary biology: the harder a signal is to fake and the more it costs, the more believable it is. The peacock’s tail is the standard example.
  • Fungibility — An economics word: any unit can be swapped for any other, like one hundred-dollar bill for another. Perry uses it for things that anyone with money can get.
  • Christopher Alexander — Architectural theorist, author of The Timeless Way of Building, A Pattern Language and The Nature of Order. Perry uses him in nearly every essay: a “pattern language” is building know-how handed down through generations; a “center” is his basic unit for describing beauty.
  • Antinatalism — The ethical position that having children is morally wrong, because life does the person born into it more harm than good.

A strange thing: after the whips were put away

In sixteenth-century France a new kind of Catholic group was spreading: penitential confraternities, brotherhoods of lay Catholics. They spread fast; by the end of the seventeenth century nearly every tiny village had its own chapter. Their central rite was collective self-flagellation, every week and three times a week during Lent.

By the early eighteenth century the brotherhoods had become “civilized.” They put down the whips and took up contemplation and charitable work. It sounds like progress. Then the brotherhoods began to decay, with more and more internal strife. Around 1731 the brothers themselves saw that discord was the problem, and their remedy was more regulation and more intricate statutes, not a return to the ritual. The historian Andrew Barnes compared the statutes before and after: “The one trend in confraternal statutes more eye-catching than the decrease in ritual devotions is the increase in procedures to regulate and reduce internal discord.” Internal discord, he notes, had not been a main concern in the statutes of earlier confraternities.

When Sarah Perry told this story in 2015, she set it under a heading that makes you laugh. She had just noted research suggesting that any picture whatsoever makes people more confident in the facts presented next to it. So she wrote, “I therefore present the most convincing argument in the world,” followed by a picture, followed by this heading:

The Institutional Power of Rhythmic Ritual: Evidence from Self-FlagellationRitual and the Consciousness Monoculture, 2015-01-08

She wasn’t only joking. Her point was that in our culture self-flagellation is mostly a punch line, and that this is “scholastic-industrial consciousness undermining the power of other modes of consciousness that it can’t readily explain or understand.” A group put away its whips, took up meditation, and fell apart, and statutes could not hold it together. A modern reader assumes the whips were the foolish part. She thought the foolish part was our not seeing what the whips were doing.

Who she is

Sarah Perry has no academic post. At the start of her book (as quoted in the Nintil review) she suspects she has made more converts by being “an adorable housewife who makes a killer chanterelle risotto” than by any argument. She fences; in 2012 she ran the trails and fire roads above Los Angeles almost daily, and by 2019 her essays have her climbing mountains around Lake Tahoe. She knits, calls herself “pathologically messy,” and mentions in passing ideas her husband suggested.

She first wrote The View from Hell as “Sister Y,” about suicide, procreation, marriage and cannabis, and later cut the blog down to six posts, leaving just the line “Old stuff.” In 2015 Venkatesh Rao invited her to write for Ribbonfarm. She became a contributing editor and wrote one essay a month for four and a half years. In August 2019 her last post explained that she had quit carbohydrates, which had cured her mental illness, and added: “I also seem to have figured out how to not write, a skill I have struggled with in the past.” She has not written anywhere I could verify since.

What she wrestles with

Start with the tagline she left on the blog’s front page: “Enjoy each other and don’t create new life.” Its two halves match the two periods of her writing.

The second half came first. In 2012 she wrote a short post on marriage law, argued like a lawyer. With no-fault divorce everywhere in America, the marriage “contract” is really an illusory contract that either party can end by saying they don’t want it anymore. A California couple once agreed in writing that whoever cheated would pay the other an extra $50,000; the husband cheated, and the court refused to enforce the agreement because it contradicted the public policy of no-fault divorce. So nobody can really promise a lifetime. Then the post turns a corner:

It is sad for all of us that the law protects the right of each person, no matter how cruel or stupid, to create new, needy, suffering human beings; but no person, no matter how mature, is trusted with the right to credibly commit him- or herself to marriage for life.The Right to Marry, 2012-07-31

Two years later she published a book, Every Cradle Is a Grave: Rethinking the Ethics of Birth and Suicide, arguing that having children is gravely immoral and that suicide is not wrong and not necessarily a product of mental illness. I could not read the book itself; below I give only the bibliographic facts and what reviewers quoted from it (see section six).

But the question she kept coming back to for a decade was not whether to live. It was a more specific one: in a time of unprecedented material plenty, why is life still so hard to bear? Our ancestors had it rougher, so what did they have around them that we have lost?

She ran into the question through her own life. In January 2012 she wrote a post about running. Every time she ran the trails above Los Angeles she wondered why, with four million people down in the city, she was the only one up there running. She cited a statistic: 86% of poor preschoolers in cities have not developed age-appropriate motor skills like running. Humans have a whole set of adaptations for running, yet in modern life running has become an eccentricity or a chore done for moral reasons. Then comes a heavy sentence:

Running high on marijuana has been the single most important factor in making life bearable for me for the past three years.Enhanced Running, 2012-01-26

The post ends with a string of questions. One asks whether the negative value of denied pleasure is different for existing versus never-existing people. The last asks what gives a sense of wanting to go forward in your story and see what happens next.

That last question is what she spent four and a half years at Ribbonfarm on. She put her answer most clearly in 2015: the ancestral solutions to material problems tended to contain solutions to higher-level problems as well. We are obese not because food is abundant but because we lost carefully evolved ancestral diets; we are lonely and bored because the cultural patterns that produced material abundance whittled away the social and psychological solutions built into the old ways. The tagline’s first half, “enjoy each other,” is the whole of her later work: trying to recover what was whittled away.

Her ideas, told as how she found them

1. Value lives in the burden: from carbon steel knives to penitent brothers

In November 2012 she started from something nobody thinks twice about: a significant share of the food people buy is never eaten and goes bad. She called that share the aspiration index: how much of what you bought was the feeling, at the moment of purchase, that you were going to become better. Kale has a high aspiration index; so do treadmills, gym memberships, musical instruments and language software. Anyone can buy them for money, but using them costs something other than money, and buyers often don’t pay it.

From there she saw a larger pattern. Some things demand a lot of their owners: knowledge, taste, care, work, membership in an insider community, sometimes pain (as with tattoos). In return they raise the owner’s value, not just as a display but by actually making you more valuable to your group. The overall drift of the modern economy is to strip away those non-monetary costs until everything is available for money alone. Her verdict:

Everything is increasingly available for money, except, I will argue, a major thing we all want to buy that gives us the feeling of meaning: our own value and specialness.Fungibility and the Loss of Demandingness, 2012-11-18

She didn’t argue in the abstract; she went through objects one at a time. The knife was one, the fountain pen another. Filling a fountain pen is “the tiniest hassle,” so the market came to sell disposable pens almost exclusively. Once nobody remembered fountain pens, disposable pens could get worse: ballpoints need so much pressure that writing becomes uncomfortable. The pain of refilling was replaced, as she puts it, with the pain of writing with substandard pens.

Three years later she moved the same structure onto one of anthropology’s oldest puzzles: why do humans make so many sacrifices that look irrational? Animal sacrifice, destroying wealth at a potlatch, keeping the Sabbath, fasting, dancing all night.

Her lever was again costly signaling: the bigger the sacrifice, the more believable the loyalty. But she used a study to show this was only half the answer. Richard Sosis and Eric Bressler measured the longevity of nineteenth-century American communes. Religious communes that demanded more sacrifice of their members (food taboos, sexual restrictions) lasted longer, but secular communes had poor survival no matter how much they demanded. The difference is that religious ritual evokes a sense of the numinous that those who feel it perceive as undeniably true; secular ritual lacks that feeling, and its ideology can be checked against experience, so its power to build trust is short-lived. She arrived at a three-part formula:

If cooperation and the solution of coordination problems is the "fire" of ritual, then costly signaling is its fuel, and the ritual mental state is its oxygen.What is Ritual?, 2015-02-11

Her hardest step concerns pain. The anthropologist Harvey Whitehouse studies “dysphoric rituals,” such as initiations and boot camps, that use fear, pain and humiliation to fuse a small group into a single identity. Her conclusion:

The unpleasantness is not a side effect to be eliminated, but precisely the source of the rituals' power.What is Ritual?, 2015-02-11

She gave “identity fusion” a more graspable name: group proprioception, perceiving your small group as an extension of your own body. Her example is a Marine Corps boot camp trick. In the middle of screaming at a worthless group of recruits, the drill instructor casually lets the unit’s flag fall, and in almost every case one of the recruits dives to keep it from touching the ground, though nobody has told them this is expected.

Back to the penitent brothers. Through this formula their decline is no longer mysterious: when they put the whips away, they cut off fuel and oxygen at once. Statutes are only words, and words can’t prove sincerity; a ritual performed in front of everyone is itself the commitment. Knives and pens in 2012, whips and communes in 2015: it is the same claim. People confirm their own value through what they give, and modern life has taken away the chances to give one by one.

2. The self is spread across other people’s heads: why ritual is a social vitamin

The second idea starts somewhere more private: the mirror. Mirrors only became common in the nineteenth century, she reminds us; before that they were luxuries of the rich. When the anthropologist Edmund Carpenter first showed mirrors to the Biami of Papua New Guinea, they were frightened, then paralyzed: they covered their mouths, hid their heads, and stood transfixed by their own images, with only their stomach muscles betraying great tension. Carpenter called it “the tribal terror of self-recognition.” Her verdict was blunt:

Access to mirrors is a novelty, and likely a harmful one.Ritual and the Consciousness Monoculture, 2015-01-08

Why would a first look in the mirror be upsetting? Her explanation comes from Philippe Rochat’s Others in Mind, “one of my favorite books of all time.” Rochat holds that human self-consciousness is social at the root: children are constrained toward awareness of themselves by others, and we learn to see ourselves from others’ point of view and to manage our image in their minds. This form of consciousness has a tragic structural flaw. The first-person self is inflated and idealized; the third-person self, the one imagined in other people’s eyes, is deflated. The gap between them never closes, and a mirror delivers exactly the accurate third-person information.

Modern life is full of mirrors: schools rank you by grades, peers rank you by what the sociologist Randall Collins calls “erotic ranking,” and photography and social networks do the rest. Meanwhile, as the psychologist Roy Baumeister argues, religion, shared morality and tradition are fading as sources of meaning, and the self must bear the weight of meaning alone. Her diagnosis: the self has become heavier and more painful, and the rituals that let people set it down for a while disappeared over the same period.

She called rituals “social vitamins”: supplied by ancestral social diets, often left out of modern lives, and their absence a kind of malnutrition. She cites the neuroscientist Arne Dietrich’s “transient hypofrontality hypothesis”: freestyling rappers and improvising jazz musicians both show reduced activity in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and dreaming, endurance running, meditation and drug states share this pattern. She casts that region as Schiller’s “watcher at the gates of the mind,” the censor that examines every idea and keeps you from letting go. Rhythmic group ritual can send the watcher off duty for a while.

Then she carried the whole thing into the Ribbonfarm essay her later posts cite most, “The Essence of Peopling.” Instead of the noun “people” she uses the verb “peopling,” the whole process of humans going about their business, and goes looking for its core.

Her way of finding the core is very her: look at how it breaks. Neurology has a class of “monothematic delusions,” usually caused by stroke or brain injury, and nearly all of them are about identity. In the Cotard delusion you believe you are dead; in the Capgras delusion you believe your spouse has been replaced by an impostor; in mirrored-self misidentification you believe the person in the mirror is not you. Even the most common delusions, grandeur and persecution, are misjudgments of one’s status and relationships. Her inference: a large part of our cognitive machinery, of which we are not normally aware, works day and night to maintain our own identities and other people’s.

The individual is not the appropriate unit of peopling, but it is the only unit that the tiling systems understand.The Essence of Peopling, 2015-04-08

Each self is spread out among many people, simulated in all their brains at varying levels of detail. You are one person with your friends and another with your grandparents, one at work and another in bed with a spouse, and none of these is the “true self”; the self exists in all of them and in the transitions between them. Collapsing them into one public self would be close to social death. People do this mutual simulation with two tools, language and ritual, and ritual carries what language can’t: hard-to-fake signals of commitment, dependability, harmony and cooperative intent.

The opposite case is bureaucracy. When you deal with the phone company or a government agency, the person across the desk is not someone simulating you but a skinsuit the institution wears. Top-down rules and scripts govern what they do, discretion is removed as far as possible, and even if they did understand you, the outcome wouldn’t change. She saw this kind of interface spreading: national curriculum standards mean children spend year after year dealing with a script rather than with a person who models them.

The essay ends with the gentlest passage she wrote:

In conclusion, drink tea, together with your friends; pay attention to the tea, and to your friends, and pay attention to your friends paying attention to the tea. Therein lies the meaning of life.The Essence of Peopling, 2015-04-08

The joke compresses the whole theory: human consciousness is, at bottom, me thinking about what you are thinking, and good ritual makes that mutual simulation pleasant instead of painful. But she also warned that ritual deserves the respect we give powerful psychoactive drugs. In a hypermobile age there is a chicken-and-egg problem, since ritual needs community and community needs ritual, and the experience of ritual cut off from a real community may be pleasant but ultimately unfulfilling. “The rebirth of ritual is the most deadly serious play.”

3. Mess is an unkept promise of order: from walls and shawls to deep laziness

The third idea starts with an aesthetic complaint: why are American cities so ugly, and why can’t anyone do anything about it? In March 2015 she answered it in a long essay whose title says a lot: “Gardens Need Walls.”

She looked at biology first. Islands are cut off by ocean, so strange things evolve there that exist nowhere else: drifting daisies evolve into trees; crabs grow a meter long and figure out how to open coconuts. When the boundary opens, usually thanks to humans, the island species are often outcompeted by species from the diversity-flattening zones of large landmasses. The cell membrane is a boundary too, and the hardest problem in evolving it was how to get things through. Boundaries are necessary, and they must be permeable.

Then came her lever: Christopher Alexander’s 1964 Notes on the Synthesis of Form. Alexander imagines a ten-by-ten grid of lightbulbs, each a variable in a design problem, on for misfit and off for fit, wired to one another. If every variable is connected to every other, trial and error will almost never turn all the lights off; only when the grid contains zones that are densely connected inside and sparsely connected to each other can the problem be split and solved piece by piece. A boundary is that kind of division: it lets what is inside solve a smaller, more solvable problem.

Alexander gives an example she quotes at length. Slovakian peasants were once famous for shawls woven from yarn in homemade dyes. Early in the twentieth century they got aniline dyes, brighter and in far more colors, and at once the shawls turned crude and uninteresting. Alexander’s explanation is that the weavers had no special gift: they could recognize a bad shawl, and within a long tradition they changed a little at a time until it was good. Faced suddenly with many more choices and asked to invent forms from scratch, their mastery disappeared. From this Perry drew her heaviest line:

Individual imagination is weak; evolution is strong.Gardens Need Walls: On Boundaries, Ritual, and Beauty, 2015-03-04

Her most memorable examples are about ugliness. One is the leaf blower: millions of people endure loud, unpleasant noise every day so that leaves can be cleared a bit more cheaply, and there is no mechanism for the suffering to be charged to those who benefit. “Ugliness is an easy externality to impose because it is difficult for individuals to coordinate against.” The other is “word pollution”: she posted Johan Christian Dahl’s 1839 painting View of Dresden by Moonlight, and then the same painting covered in advertising text. Literate people can’t help reading words in their field of view, and the beauty is ruined.

Two years later she went from ugliness to mess. “Tendrils of Mess in our Brains” opens with a photo of her own workspace, which she says is almost as embarrassing as posting nudes. She rejects the fashionable idea that mess is good, and she refuses the explanation that humans are just bad (“a boring answer”). Instead she starts from an observation by Alan Watts: clouds are not a mess, foam on water is not a mess; they are not symmetrical, yet clearly not a mess. An ashtray full of cigarette butts is.

Her first candidate was entropy from information theory, and she knocked it down herself (see the figure). A solid black square has the lowest entropy and is not a mess; random noise has the highest and is not a mess either; a square of overlapping letters and logos takes fewer bits than the noise and is a mess. A mossy stone ruin is not a mess; a rectilinear painted room of drywall in ruins is.

So what is the answer? For mess to appear, she says, its components must imply an extreme kind of order, the regular order usually associated with human intention. Flat surfaces and printed text promise a particular order; mess is that promise broken, subverted by other, competing orders.

Mess is only perceptible because it produces in our minds an imaginary order that is missing.Tendrils of Mess in our Brains, 2017-01-05

It is as if every object sends out invisible tendrils saying, “the matter around me should be ordered in some particular way.” The stronger the claims and the more they conflict, the more mess. This explains a great deal. A rectilinear Le Corbusier house is especially prone to mess; its residents say they had to give up ornate antiques and comfortable armchairs. A city under snow looks less messy because the snow masks the competing claims of billboards, cars and dumpsters. Put a photo of basement clutter through a kaleidoscope filter and it stops being a mess, because the kaleidoscope imposes what Alexander calls local symmetries.

In 2018 she took the line to its end in “Deep Laziness.” It begins with an imagined person: a very lazy shop owner whose customers are all quite satisfied, or some kind of Taoist fisherman. Perhaps, she suggests, the universe has this personality. Alexander’s The Nature of Order supplies the deeper layer: nature generates through “structure-preserving transformations,” each step taking its cue from existing structure, strengthening it and elaborating it, instead of imposing a foreign, conflicting order. Clouds are never a mess because they are products of such processes.

This is the laziest way to do things, so that is how the universe does it.Deep Laziness, 2018-04-06

Then she carried the architectural theory into daily life. A person’s behavioral repertoire is small; bored, most people have about three to five things they do, and those few things are the texture of a life. Her own “centers” are running and knitting, but, she says, “one person’s ritual is all but useless to another, especially a really good ritual.” A good ritual is adapted bit by bit for one particular person, and the only test is how it feels. Her example: in deep winter it is too cold for her favorite pre-dawn run. She tried warmer clothes and switching to afternoons, and in the end a gym treadmill preserved what she wanted from the summer dawn run, then sprouted yoga and swinging a weight ball as well. She calls this “elaborated time,” as opposed to “scheduled time”: organizing yourself top-down to fit a pre-existing image of proper behavior.

Walls, mess, laziness: one idea in three steps. Good order is not designed; it grows inside boundaries, along structure that is already there. The modern problem is that we keep trying to impose a plan from the top, on the world and on ourselves.

How she thinks

Pick something nobody takes seriously and treat it as a puzzle. Kitchen knives, fountain pens, mess, greetings, leaf blowers. At the extreme, in “Puzzle Theory,” she ran a “fan theory” on the first microwave oven manual PDF she could find online. “Panasonic Inverter” anagrams to “Scanner, invite a pro!”; “IMPORTANT SAFETY INSTRUCTIONS” anagrams to “Transform tiny spots in Tau Ceti”; the yes/no column of the cookware table is binary that, converted, points to a news story about a habitable planet around Tau Ceti. It is a joke, but beneath it is a theory she meant: humor exposes a covertly introduced false belief, while a fan theory builds a surprising interpretation on ambiguous material. “Humor is destructive; fan theory is constructive.” Scientific theories and theological interpretations, to her, are fan theories of reality itself.

Infer what a function normally does from how it breaks. From the Cotard and Capgras delusions she inferred that the brain works constantly at maintaining identity; from the brothers’ quarrels after the whips were put away, that ritual holds groups together; from the sudden ugliness of Slovakian shawls, that beauty is evolved rather than drawn by genius.

The anthropologist’s list of human universals is her standing lever. She keeps citing Donald Brown’s Human Universals: hairstyles are universal, string and tying material are universal, music related to dance and religion is universal. If every human society has something, she takes it to be good for people and asks whether modern life still supplies it.

One borrowed framework, pushed all the way. Christopher Alexander is her master key: ritual, cities, mess and personal routine all get his pattern languages, centers and structure-preserving transformations. She admits her thinking about “centers” changes every time she writes about them, “hopefully becoming more correct.” The reading list she published in 2015 splits into “core” and “peripheral” frameworks, and the first two core entries are Alexander’s.

Use yourself as the sample, and say so. Running, fencing, being high, knitting, a messy desk: all of it goes straight into the argument. Her 2013 guide to cannabis for nerds says its most important advice is to establish communication with your sober self: before getting high, head a sheet of paper “Dear sober self” and fill it in while high. It is also how she works: treating different states of consciousness as modules that can pass messages.

Her view of citation. She wrote a line that suits this site:

Every citation is either a homework assignment or a promise.Why Books Are Fake, 2017-06-01

Her essays are full of long quotations, but nearly all of them are promises: she brings you the part of the source that matters, then tells you why it matters.

Where she might be wrong

She wrote the sharpest criticism herself. In June 2019 at Refactor Camp she gave a talk on how to see voids. Its hook was the bright yellow wolf lichen growing in rings around the pines of the Tahoe National Forest, and the answer was that sapsuckers, a small woodpecker, peck rings of holes in the trees first and the lichen colonizes them. The story supported her theme perfectly. Then she climbed more mountains. Some mountains had trees covered in lichen and the neighboring ones had none; some lichen grew only along the boughs, some wound through the cracks of rough bark, and fallen fronds on the trail suggested it didn’t need a hole at all. She tried to check the sapsuckers’ range and couldn’t.

Unfortunately, I think my almost-certainly-wrong answer is more satisfying than the truth (as I am able to ascertain it).Meaning as Ambiguity, 2019-08-15

From this she named the risk in her whole genre. Puzzle-box art rests on the premise that a discoverable hidden order lies underneath, and reality, lacking an author with a mind like ours, makes no such promise. “The softer the science - the more layers of emerging complexity it tries to account for - the more dubious the constructed order.” That reads almost as a verdict on most of her own work. The penitent brothers, the communes, the shawls are all beautiful solutions, and they all come from exactly the soft fields she means.

Thin evidence, picked a little too neatly. Her big claims often rest on one story or one small study. The boot-camp flag trick comes without a source; the fMRI study of freestyle rappers is, in her own words, “an even smaller study.” She jokes about this, but a joke is not a test. The confraternity story has other explanations too: eighteenth-century French Catholicism was changing, and so were the Enlightenment and relations between crown and church. Putting away the whips and the brothers’ decline may be two results of the same tide rather than cause and effect.

The ancestral environment is too convenient a screen. Most of her arguments take the form: ancestors had X, modern life lost X, so we suffer. But we know little about ancestral environments, and almost any modern ache can be projected onto them. She calls schooling a “free child caging service,” a stance that is plain to see; the other side of the ledger, vaccines, literacy, child mortality, she barely tallies.

She is too light about the people who get trapped. In the marriage post she concedes that enforced marriage “would force some to remain in bad, even abusive, marriages,” then waves it off: “As generally occurs in human institutions, a few unlucky folks are thrown under the bus for the good of the other monkeys.” But who gets thrown under the bus, and how many, is exactly the question no-fault divorce was meant to answer.

The book. I could not read Every Cradle Is a Grave (Nine-Banded Books, 2014) itself, only two reviews. The Nintil review quotes its opening: she will argue that “life—not just human life, but all life capable of having experiences—is very bad,” that it is “very immoral” to have babies, and that suicide is “an ethically privileged, rational response to the badness of life”; Nintil also questions the book’s view that social bonds are what keep people alive. Another reviewer, David R. MacIver, expected to be a sympathetic reader and came away less sympathetic to antinatalism than when he started. His criticism:

I felt there were a lot of unexamined assumptions, and the entire thing read like a cost-benefit analysis without the benefit analysis part.David R. MacIver, Book Review: Every Cradle is a Grave by Sarah Perry, 2019-01-24

He also found the empirical claims “a bit weak and cherry-picked in places.” I can’t judge from here whether that is fair to the book. In the Ribbonfarm essays I read, she no longer writes about the ethics of procreation; she writes about what makes living easier to bear.

Crossovers

  • Complexity science · Kauffman’s NK model: Stuart Kauffman models fitness landscapes with N genes, each interacting with K others. The larger K, the more rugged the landscape, the more local optima, and the more easily evolution gets stuck, which he calls a “complexity catastrophe.” It is the same structure as the Alexander lightbulb grid Perry borrows: with too many connections trial and error finds no good solution, and modularity has to push K down. Where it fits: it turns her “boundary” from a metaphor into a parameter. Where it doesn’t: the NK model is only about search difficulty, not beauty or belonging. Perry’s boundaries hold rituals and small groups; Kauffman’s landscape holds only fitness.
  • Neuroscience · the default mode network: a set of brain regions active when we dwell on ourselves. Brewer and colleagues (2011) found it less active in experienced meditators, and Carhart-Harris and colleagues (2012) found that psilocybin reduces its activity too. That looks a lot like her watcher at the gates. Where it doesn’t fit: she relied on Dietrich’s prefrontal hypothesis, which points at a different region.
  • Buddhism · non-self (anattā): she says the modern self is too heavy and needs ritual to set it down for a while; Buddhism holds that the self is itself a process of interdependent conditions. Both treat letting go of the self as relief. But Buddhism asks you to see through the self by insight, for good; she wants a temporary dissolution in the group, after which you go home to the same self.

Ten takeaways

  1. What a thing demands of its owner beyond money is exactly what lets it raise the owner’s value; when the market removes those costs, the reward goes too.
  2. The “aspiration index”: the share of a purchase that goes unused measures how much was bought for the feeling of becoming better, not for the thing itself.
  3. The ritual formula: cooperation is the fire, costly sacrifice the fuel, the evoked mental state the oxygen, and without either of the last two nothing burns.
  4. Sosis’s data: the more demanding a religious commune, the longer it lasted, while secular communes failed however demanding they were; the difference was a felt sense of the sacred.
  5. The pain and humiliation of dysphoric rituals are not side effects but the force that fuses a small group into “group proprioception.”
  6. Human self-consciousness is forced on us by others; the first-person self is overrated, the third-person self underrated, and mirrors and rankings keep exposing the gap.
  7. A bureaucrat is a skinsuit the institution wears, following a script instead of modeling you, and this interface is spreading into schools.
  8. Sarah Perry holds that individual imagination is weak and evolution strong: Slovakian shawls turned ugly with aniline dyes because beauty is refined bit by bit inside boundaries.
  9. Mess is not high entropy but fragments whose implied orders conflict and go unkept; clouds are never a mess because nature only makes structure-preserving transformations.
  10. Perry admits her “almost-certainly-wrong” woodpecker answer is more satisfying than the truth: the softer the science, the more dubious the beautiful order built on it.

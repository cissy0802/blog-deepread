blog deep-read · 01

Leo Wang · MacroAlpha

Reading 2,500 years of contemplative practice as an engineering spec · blog.leowang.net

What he is wrestling with

Twenty-five centuries ago, people with no instruments at all sat cross-legged, looked inward, and wrote a complete manual for how the mind works. Wang Lijie — who writes as Leo Wang — asks one question of it: which parts are real findings, and which are rust that later hands added?

He refuses both of the usual camps. The apologist says the ancients were right and you should simply believe. The debunker says it is pre-scientific fantasy. He takes a third road: treat every traditional practice as an unverified engineering spec, and ask, item by item, why it works and what is actually happening in the brain while you do it.

His three answers

1. Practice is not morality; it is neural engineering. The six pāramitās — giving, precepts, patience, diligence, meditation, wisdom — are not a list of virtues but, in his words, "a systematic repair plan aimed at six of the most stubborn factory defects in the human brain." And the order cannot be shuffled: if the foundation is not sound, what you build on top becomes a new attachment.

2. Awareness is a neutral tool — it can save you, and it can be turned around to lock you in. Common sense says the more aware you are, the harder you are to fool. He argues the opposite: the people captured deepest by coercive spiritual groups tend to be the educated ones with years of sitting practice. Awareness is consciousness's rear-view mirror — and if someone fits that mirror with distorting glass, the better your technique, the more dangerous it gets.

3. Intellectual history is a history of engineering revisions — and proving a story was invented raises its value rather than lowering it. Zen's most beautiful story, the Flower Sermon, he dates on three lines of evidence to Song-dynasty authorship — and then argues it is better for being invented: whoever wrote it could have chosen a more marketable mystical version, and instead chose a flower and a smile.

The example that best shows him

Nāgārjuna used logic to dismantle the assumption that things have independent essence. An opponent asked: does your blade itself have independent essence? Nāgārjuna answered that his blade too is empty — and it can cut precisely because it is empty. Wang adds a kitchen-level footnote: medicine clears out what is surplus in your body and is then metabolised away. "If a pill you swallow cannot be metabolised and stays in your body forever, it is no longer medicine — it is a stone."

He does not stop at admiration. In the same piece he goes on to dismantle what Nāgārjuna left behind: a precise diagnosis with no prescription — "between knowing that all is empty and living as though all is empty lies an enormous chasm." Explain something fully, then take apart what you just explained — that double move is his most reliable habit.

How to read him

Start with "Zen's Most Beautiful Story Is Probably a Fabrication"; the whole method is in it. For his hardest work, read the six-pāramitās piece. For his most honest, read "The Deeper You Meditate, the Easier You Are to Brainwash?" — that one turns the knife on his own field.

Two warnings: a substantial share of the site's posts sit behind a paid membership wall (the three above are free); and he is a practitioner looking for mechanisms behind practice, not a neutral science communicator.

01In one sentence

He reads 2,500 years of contemplative tradition as an unverified engineering spec: never asking whether to believe it, only why it works and what happens in the brain — and turning the same coldness on where it fails and where later hands added to it.

Glossary

  • Six pāramitās: the six Mahāyāna trainings — giving (dāna), precepts (śīla), patience (kṣānti), diligence (vīrya), meditation (dhyāna), wisdom (prajñā).
  • Svabhāva: the independent essence by which a thing would stand on its own. Madhyamaka's whole project is to argue it does not exist; śūnyatā ("emptiness") therefore means not "nothing" but "no independent self-existing essence."
  • Yogācāra: the Buddhist school that arose after Nāgārjuna, holding that the world we experience is generated by the mind system. Its ālaya-vijñāna is a posited "seed store"; its svasaṃvedana is the layer that knows it is knowing — roughly today's metacognition.
  • Default mode network: the self-narrating system the brain runs automatically when idle.
  • Predictive coding: the theory that the brain generates predictions first and corrects them with sensory data, rather than receiving signals passively.

03What he is wrestling with

The root question he keeps returning to, he turned into an explicit measuring stick in his Nāgārjuna series: "Compared with what the Buddha saw under the bodhi tree, is this closer or further away?" After each thinker and each sutra, he comes back and measures against it.

Unpacked: with no brain imaging and no controlled trials, how much did the ancients actually see through introspection alone? The question is rare because it refuses both easier postures — apologetics (your doubt only shows you have not practised enough) and cheap debunking (pre-scientific fantasy). What he wants is to break traditional practice into specific claims that modern evidence can test, then test them one by one, and say plainly which survive and which do not.

04The core ideas, in full

Idea 1: Practice is an engineering fix for the brain's "factory defects," and can be traced to the circuit level

How he argues it. He pins each pāramitā to a neural mechanism with experimental support (see the diagram), each with a specific study: giving cites Zak's 2007 intranasal-oxytocin experiment (generosity roughly 80% above controls); precepts cite Gollwitzer's meta-analysis of "implementation intentions" (94 studies, 8,000-plus subjects, effect size about 0.65); meditation cites the Davidson and Lutz 2004 PNAS work on gamma activity.

"These six are not six virtues picked at random. They are a systematic repair plan aimed at six of the most stubborn factory defects in the human brain." — "Why Do the Six Pāramitās Work? Neuroscience Has an Answer" · blog.leowang.net (translation mine)

Why it is counterintuitive. "Buddhism and science converge" is a cheap platitude anyone can offer. His claim is far stronger and far riskier: the number and the order of the six are dictated by the brain's architecture — "drop any layer and the system has a hole. Add one? You can't." That rewrites a moral checklist as an architecture document.

He runs the same move on the Śūraṅgama Sūtra: the Buddha asks seven times where the mind is and demolishes all seven of Ānanda's answers. Wang reads this not as sophistry but as an attack on the premise that mind is an object with a location — the same wall neuroscience keeps hitting when it hunts for the seat of consciousness ("the premise that consciousness is an entity that can be located is, I suspect, wrong from the start"). What is rare is that he brakes on himself: "every scientific correspondence I have drawn today is a structural isomorphism, not an equivalence."

Idea 2: Awareness is a neutral instrument, and it can be turned around to lock you in

How he argues it. He first gives "awareness" a precise definition from the Yogācāra four-part analysis — the object seen, the act of seeing, the knowing that one is seeing, and the confirmation of that knowing; meditation training is the strengthening of the third. Then he notes that this capacity is neutral in itself: it is only a mirror, and what matters is not how bright the mirror is but what is placed in front of it. He then brings in Lifton's "mystical manipulation" and lays out a three-step capture: give you something real (genuine meditation instruction, real benefit, trust established); swap the foundations underneath; then lock you in with the awareness you yourself trained — you notice the urge to leave, and your awareness tells you that urge is fear, and fear means your practice is insufficient.

"Your awareness has not weakened. Your awareness is stronger than it has ever been. But it is now operating on a distorted foundation. Like that warped rear-view mirror: the clearer it gets, the more dangerous it is." — "The Deeper You Meditate, the Easier You Are to Brainwash?" · blog.leowang.net (translation mine)

Why it is counterintuitive. In groups that go wrong, the core members share a profile: highly educated, capable of independent thought, and already meditating before they joined. He also cites Britton's group at Brown: roughly 58% of participants in mindfulness courses reported adverse experiences with negative affect.

What gives this piece its weight is that it is a writer on contemplative practice deliberately writing about how contemplative practice harms people. He offers three workable tests: does it encourage you to leave; is your social world widening or narrowing; is there a person who may not be questioned. The conclusion is one line: "The awareness is yours, but the direction it faces may not be."

Idea 3: Intellectual history is a history of revisions — and "this was invented" adds value

How he argues it. He reads intellectual history the way one reads version history: Nāgārjuna fixed the bug of inherent essence — "Madhyamaka is not an innovation, it is a repair" — but the fix planted two new hazards: a precise diagnosis with no operational path, and "emptiness" abused into nihilism. The unanswered question he left (where does difference come from, what carries experience?) directly produced the next version, Yogācāra.

"Every great answer becomes the starting point of a larger question. Nāgārjuna cleared the foundations, but what he left was an empty lot." — "Nāgārjuna's Remedy Cured the Old Illness and Planted a New One" · blog.leowang.net (translation mine)

The same method is sharper still on textual criticism. The Flower Sermon is Zen's origin story, and he lays out three lines of evidence: none of the first six patriarchs ever mentioned it; the complete scene first reaches paper in a lamp-transmission record of 1036 CE, roughly 1,500 years after the Buddha's death; and the sutra it is supposedly drawn from was judged a Chinese composition by the Japanese scholar Nukariya Kaiten.

Why it is counterintuitive. Religious source-criticism usually has only two exits: the apologist says it is true, the debunker says it is false and therefore worthless. He stands in neither — it is invented, and it is deeper precisely because it is invented: whoever wrote it could have chosen a more marketable version (a secret initiation, a mantra, a portent) and instead chose the most anti-formulaic thing available, one flower and one smile.

"He used a false story to capture, with precision, a real problem. Some things shatter the moment language touches them." — "Zen's Most Beautiful Story Is Probably a Fabrication" · blog.leowang.net (translation mine)

He then connects that real problem to Polanyi's 1966 tacit knowledge: you can recognise a friend's face but cannot say what distinguishes it. Kāśyapa smiled not because he grasped a doctrine but because he jumped from the description of the flower to the flower. Which loops him back to a harder line: "Knowledge is a door, and it is also a wall."

05How he thinks

1. Swap "should I believe it" for "how does it run." Faced with any traditional practice he asks only for mechanism: what happens in the brain while you do this? That single substitution turns a question of faith into a technical question that can be argued.

2. Every abstraction gets a kitchen-level analogy, and the analogy is load-bearing rather than decorative. Medicine versus a kidney stone (emptiness is itself empty); a rear-view mirror fitted with distorting glass (captured awareness); a virus disguised as a system file paired with a diligent antivirus program (seeds in the store-consciousness paired with trained self-awareness); mistaking a black slipper for a rat at night (predictive coding). His arguments advance on analogies, not on terminology.

3. Give years, sample sizes and effect sizes, never "studies show." Zak 2007; Gollwitzer's 94 studies and 0.65; PNAS 2004; Britton's 58%. In Chinese-language writing on practice this is very rare.

4. Draw the boundary himself, at the moment his argument is strongest. "A structural isomorphism, not an equivalence." Before discussing the Śūraṅgama Sūtra he states that its authenticity is still disputed and that he will not settle it in that piece.

5. End on a question or a small experiment, not a conclusion. Spend three seconds looking at an object without naming it; recall a belief you hold absolutely, and ask whether you reasoned your way to it or were taught it. The verification is handed back to the reader.

6. Keep one measuring stick across the whole series, returned to every issue. It is what keeps several hundred daily posts from disintegrating into fragments.

06Limits and disputes

Output and process. The great majority of the 800-plus posts come from under a year since autumn 2025, at two to three a day. He describes himself as a "vibe coder" and an "AI agent driver," and the site carries sections on prompts and workflows. This is an AI-assisted content pipeline: broad coverage, tidy structure, and the cost is that a reader cannot easily tell which sentence he thought through himself and which the pipeline produced. His best passages are precisely the ones carrying first-person experience.

The paywall. Of six posts I sampled, three showed only their opening two sentences; the rest required paid membership. For a free reader, "read this and you have read him" only half holds.

Selective evidence, and a slippery isomorphism. He cites almost exclusively supporting studies. The Davidson and Lutz 2004 sample was tiny, and the "thirty-fold gamma" figure, much quoted, has been much criticised as ungeneralisable; the biggest controversy in a decade of meditation research is small effect sizes and replication difficulty — he used the negative data in the brainwashing piece but never went back to weaken the six-pāramitās conclusion. More fundamentally, "six bottlenecks happen to cover the whole cognitive stack" is unfalsifiable: almost any set of behavioural trainings can be matched to some circuit. He states he means only structural isomorphism, yet the rhetoric throughout ("as precise as a software architecture document") implies something stronger.

Direction. He is not a neutral science communicator but a practitioner seeking mechanisms for practice: the default assumption is "the ancients were right, let us find out why," not "the ancients may have been wrong, let us look." The Flower Sermon piece proves he can invert that — but it is the exception.

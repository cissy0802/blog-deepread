Sabine Hossenfelder
The universe doesn't care whether you find it beautiful

Sabine Hossenfelder · blog Backreaction (a general-relativity term for matter acting back on the spacetime it sits in) · German theoretical physicist working on the phenomenology of quantum gravity, later famous for her YouTube channel · Still active: on the day this page was written (2026-10-23) the latest post was from 2026-10-21 · Corpus: started February 2006, 2,949 posts to date (counted from the site feed). In the early years she co-wrote it with her husband, the physicist Stefan Scherer; of the 311 posts in 2007, 174 are signed by Stefan or by both · Two turning points: from around 2020 most posts are transcripts of her YouTube videos; from around 2023 most posts are just an embedded video plus a sentence or two, with the transcripts moved to Patreon and comments permanently closed, while the posting rate rose to 300 in 2024 · So this page is based on 18 full posts under her own name from 2006–2022, plus a handful of the short post-2023 blurbs.

In November 2018, Sabine Hossenfelder did a sum for physics. According to membership data from the American and German physical societies, the number of physicists grew roughly a hundredfold between 1900 and 2000, and the growth was exponential. Assume physicists today work as many hours as they did a century ago. Then 14 months of total working time starting today equals 40 years starting a hundred years ago.

Going by working hours only, physicists today should be able to do in 14 months what a century earlier took 40 years.The present phase of stagnation in the foundations of physics is not normal, 2018-11-19

Those 40 years a century ago brought special and general relativity, quantum mechanics and antimatter. The last 40 years brought no change to the theories at the foundation of physics. The standard model was complete by the mid-1970s, and every experiment since has simply confirmed it. She admits the sum shouldn't be taken seriously. She did it to kill a familiar excuse: "Decades without progress are normal. It took two thousand years to get from the idea of atoms to detecting them."

Most people who see this stagnation blame nature: the energies are too high and the experiments too expensive. Hossenfelder blames something else. What broke, she says, is not the universe but the way physicists pick theories. For decades, theorists used beauty, simplicity and "naturalness" to decide which ideas were worth testing. Naturalness says the numbers in a theory shouldn't depend on unlikely coincidences. By that standard, the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) had to find something besides the Higgs boson, such as supersymmetric particles. In 2016 the verdict came in: the Higgs, and nothing else.

Nature, it turns out, doesn’t like to be told what’s presumably natural.The LHC “nightmare scenario” has come true, 2016-08-06

Her case against naturalness fits in one sentence, and it is almost embarrassingly simple: you can't call something unlikely without a probability distribution. If you find a neat stack of balanced stones on a hiking trail, you conclude that someone built it. You can, because you've seen thousands of stones left by weathering and know how they usually land. But we have only ever seen one universe with one set of constants. On what grounds do we call that set unlikely? The "uniform distribution" hiding behind the argument has no data behind it. As early as 2009 she wrote that our sense of a "normal" size comes from evolution: the bears our ancestors hunted didn't vary by more than a few orders of magnitude. That post ended with one line: "The universe just doesn't care whether you like it."

The second step is the real backbone of her blog. If the method so plainly fails, why does nobody change it? She wrote her answer in 2008, ten years before the book that made her famous, Lost in Math. In a market, the public learns within days whether a candy bar is any good. A theory in physics takes five to ten years to develop and decades to test, and until then the only people who can judge it are other physicists.

Until an idea is developed sufficiently and can be experimentally tested, we have only ourselves to judge on each other.We have only ourselves to judge on each other, 2008-07-13

That creates a closed loop: money goes where people go, people go where money goes, and interest follows both. Ten years later she put it more harshly. They review each other's papers and each other's grant proposals, and they tell each other it is good science. From the inside it looks like business as usual. Nothing comes out of it.

So what should physicists do? She went through the history of physics looking for what successful predictions had in common. Einstein, Dirac and the people who predicted the Higgs were all repairing a logical inconsistency in the theories of their day. None of them was trying to make a theory prettier. So her prescription is this: stop solving non-problems like ugliness, and work on real contradictions, such as the unquantized gravitational field or the quantum measurement problem. Her own bet, superdeterminism, is a road almost nobody takes, and the same rule led her there.

She has also written down where the rule stops. In 2022 she announced that she had quit working on the black hole information paradox. It is a real contradiction, but there are too many candidate answers and no experiment that could choose among them: "Physics isn't math." A contradiction is not enough. You also need a way to measure the answer.

So her central point is this: when a field has no data, taste quietly passes itself off as evidence, and the people who should correct that taste are the same people funded by it. She calls particle physics "the canary in the coal mine." As experiments get more expensive and rarer, other fields will run into the same problem.

Glossary

  • Standard Model — the theory of all known elementary particles and of three of the four forces (not gravity). It was complete by the mid-1970s, and every experiment since agrees with it.
  • Large Hadron Collider (LHC) — CERN's 27-kilometer particle collider. It started in 2008 and found the Higgs boson in 2012.
  • Beyond the Standard Model (BSM) / phenomenology — BSM means new physics the Standard Model doesn't include. Phenomenology is the branch of theoretical physics that translates theories into "what should an experiment see." Hossenfelder calls herself a phenomenologist.
  • Naturalness / fine-tuning — the rule that a theory's dimensionless constants should be close to 1, not the result of two huge numbers almost exactly cancelling. When a theory breaks the rule it is called "fine-tuned," which is taken as a sign that it is incomplete.
  • Supersymmetry (SUSY) — a theory that gives every known particle a "superpartner." One of its main selling points was restoring naturalness. The LHC has not found a single superpartner.
  • Multiverse — the idea that our universe is one of countless universes, each with different constants.
  • Quantum measurement problem — in quantum mechanics the wave function evolves smoothly until a measurement, when it suddenly "collapses" to one result. Nobody can say how these two rules fit together or what the collapse actually is.
  • Bell's theorem / statistical independence — Bell proved that hidden-variable theories meeting certain assumptions can only produce correlations up to a limit, and experiments exceed that limit. One assumption is statistical independence: what the experimenter chooses to measure is unrelated to the state of the particles being measured.
  • Superdeterminism — a deterministic theory that drops statistical independence. The detector settings and the measured system are not independent, so local hidden variables can reproduce quantum mechanics.
  • Black hole information paradox — Hawking found that black holes radiate and eventually evaporate, and the radiation carries no information. Quantum mechanics says information can't simply disappear.

A strange thing: a prophecy from 2008, and a rejection letter

In July 2008, Hossenfelder was still a postdoc and would be job-hunting again that autumn. She asked a string theorist how the job market looked in his field. Bad, he said. People were losing interest because there hadn't been enough progress, and he himself was turning to loop quantum gravity. She went home and wrote a long essay on how academia differs from a market. Halfway through it she made a prediction. Once everyone sees that the current way of evaluating research doesn't work, she wrote, people will rush toward quick fixes, such as rewarding "independent" and "alternative" researchers, or rewarding phenomenology. But the selection pressure would remain, and people would just learn to satisfy the new criteria:

In two decades from now, somebody will come and say: Hey, there is trouble in physics. They are working on all of these cheap little pheno-models, how is ever something supposed to come out of this?We have only ourselves to judge on each other, 2008-07-13

She was a phenomenologist herself. Ten years later, the somebody who stood up and said physics was in trouble was also her.

The same post ends with a short "Meta" paragraph. She had applied to the Foundational Questions Institute (FQXi) for money to study exactly these questions, and had been turned down. She posted the reason verbatim: the proposal seemed to "overlap with studies of the sociology of science, which is a well-developed field, but one in which the principal investigator appears to have no direct professional experience or training." Her reply: "That is entirely correct. I am just a physicist who has had too much time thinking how the academic system sucks, wondering why nobody in it seems to listen to what the sociologists say."

The letter said she wasn't qualified to study how scientists judge one another. Ten years later she wrote a whole book on the subject.

Who she is

Hossenfelder was born in Frankfurt in 1976 and earned her doctorate at the University of Frankfurt. Her thesis was on the production of microscopic black holes in models with large extra dimensions, exactly the kind of prediction that was hot for the LHC at the time. Postdocs followed in Arizona, Santa Barbara and at the Perimeter Institute in Canada. In 2009 she became an assistant professor at Nordita in Stockholm, and in 2015 she moved back to the Frankfurt Institute for Advanced Studies. She started the blog in February 2006 with her husband, Stefan Scherer. Readers called her "Bee," and joint posts were signed "Stefan and Bee"; Stefan often wrote the posts marking famous physicists' birthdays.

Later she gave a few glimpses of her working life. In 2022 she opened a video by saying she had "ended up in the basement in front of a green screen." The same year she wrote that she was "merely one person working four jobs while raising two kids," and that her work on superdeterminism had no funding at all. Her YouTube channel now has more than 1.7 million subscribers, and the blog has become a mirror of it.

What she's wrestling with

The question she hasn't let go of in nearly twenty years is this: why have the foundations of physics made no progress in forty years, and why don't the people working there see that as a problem?

She ran into this question earlier than most people realize. In May 2006, three months into the blog, she was stuck in an airport with nothing to read but Susskind's The Cosmic Landscape, a book about the string theory "landscape" and the anthropic principle. The anthropic principle says the constants of nature are what they are because otherwise we wouldn't be here to ask. She wrote a parable. Imagine you are an atom in a liver cell of an alcoholic. You're a smart atom and you've figured out the Standard Model, and you notice that your universe, the liver, has recently been growing. You and your colleagues propose theories: a mysterious "a-essence"; "meta-livers" with different laws; a beautiful string-like winding structure that supposedly contains all the information needed to explain the liver but can't predict anything. People are calling it a crisis in liver physics. How smart would the atom have to be to imagine a man who lost his job, then his girlfriend, then the use of one leg, and then took to drink?

Retreating to the anthropic principle means to me to give up the believe that there is something to understand.The Principle of Finite Imagination, 2006-05-15

Back then she was still an optimist. The post ends: "Fortunately, it is in the nature of human beings to never be satisfied. Therefore, I have no doubt that this crisis is temporarily."

Twelve years later she had dropped the word "crisis." Things hadn't improved; the word was simply too kind:

But I don’t think “crisis” describes the current situation well: Crisis is so optimistic.The present phase of stagnation in the foundations of physics is not normal, 2018-11-19

A crisis, she explained, implies that people have realized their mistake, that change is coming, that everyone is waking up. She saw no awakening; the community's self-reflection was "zero, zilch, nada, nichts, null." So she called it stagnation instead.

Between "this crisis is temporary" in 2006 and "crisis is so optimistic" in 2018 lie the twelve years in which she moved the problem from physics to physicists. In 2006 she thought the difficulty was on nature's side and just hadn't been cracked yet. By 2018 she was sure it was on the side of the people judging the theories.

Her ideas, told as she found them

1. Naturalness isn't a scientific argument: without a probability distribution there is no "unlikely"

Physicists often call a theory "unnatural." In December 2009 she wrote a post explaining why she had always stayed away from such arguments. She started by poking fun at the word: everything we observe is in nature, so calling any of it unnatural is an oxymoron. What physicists actually mean is a dimensionless parameter much larger or much smaller than one. It has to be dimensionless, because any constant with units equals 1 in the right unit system:

The Earth weighs exactly one Earthmass. Clearly an indication for intelligent design.What is Natural?, 2009-12-17

The best-known case is the cosmological constant. Divide the fourth power of the Planck mass by the measured cosmological constant and you get 10 to the power of 120. Physicists find this number unnatural and go looking for a theory to explain it. She wanted to ask: why does it seem unnatural? Her answer drew on evolution.

"The bears your ancestors hunted down did not differ in size more than some orders of magnitude, if you go into a forest you're unlikely to find a tree 1017 meters high." We are used to things being roughly average, and our brains like simple, unifying explanations, which we call beautiful. But keep asking "why," she wrote, and the scientific ground crumbles under your feet. Because small numbers make you uneasy? Because many parameters aren't satisfying? Because it isn't pretty? She didn't deny that such intuitions are useful; they point to where current theories fall short. But:

Bottomline: The universe just doesn't care whether you like it.What is Natural?, 2009-12-17

Three weeks later she took the idea a step further, and on the way did something very typical of her. In a German popular-science magazine she read about a study by the neuropsychologist Peter Brugger. Believers in the paranormal and skeptics had to pick out words and faces from noise in a very short time. Believers more often saw a signal where there was only noise (a Type I error). Skeptics more often dismissed a real signal as noise (a Type II error). After half the participants took levodopa, which raises dopamine levels, the skeptics became as bad as the believers. She went looking for the paper. Google Scholar didn't have it, and neither did the author's website. She found dozens of articles about it that all seemed copied from each other, and none of them cited a journal. In the end she wrote to Brugger himself. He replied almost immediately: the study had never been published, more than seven years on.

That detour was only her warm-up. What she really wanted to ask was: how often do physicists see faces in noise? She offered a list. The Higgs vacuum expectation value is the geometric mean of the Planck mass and the fourth root of the cosmological constant. Koide's formula. Anomalous alignments in the cosmic microwave background. The 1.5-sigma "detection." And one reader had noticed that, in SI units, the gravitational constant times the speed of light is close to 2/100. Her conclusion:

I am concluding it is more likely we're jumping on clues that are none.Is Physics Cognitively Biased?, 2010-01-06

At this stage her position was still mild. Naturalness was "a useful guide," and large numbers really do itch. "I am not saying thou shell disregard your intuition. I say thou shell be clear what is intuition and what derivation."

Then came August 2016. A "diphoton bump" at the LHC had excited the whole field for most of a year, and then it vanished as more data came in. What particle physicists called the "nightmare scenario" had arrived: the Higgs and nothing else. She wrote that in her whole career, starting in 1995 when she finished high school and watched the top quark being discovered, all she had seen was failure. She had seen grand unified models proposed and falsified, and dark matter candidates not found, each time followed by "a ritual parameter adjustment." The masses "predicted" for supersymmetric particles had climbed from a few GeV to a few hundred GeV and then to the few TeV the LHC could reach.

What worries me much more is our failure to learn from failure.The LHC “nightmare scenario” has come true, 2016-08-06

By June 2018, around the publication of Lost in Math, she had sharpened the argument as far as it would go. Her example was a pile of stones. If you see a delicately balanced stack of stones on a walk, you conclude someone built it, because you've seen plenty of stones scattered by weathering, erosion and deposition. You have statistics. A solar system with ten planets in almost the same orbit is unlikely, while ten planets in almost the same plane is not. We know that too, because we've observed many solar systems and can derive their likely distribution from known physics. In cases like these, naturalness is a good argument. But in high-energy physics, naturalness is used to choose among theories, and there we can never have the distribution. We only ever observe one set of parameters, the one describing our universe. The implied "roughly uniform near 1" distribution is an unscientific assumption.

That you can’t speak about probabilities without a probability distribution isn’t a particularly deep insight, but I’ve had a hard time getting particle physicists to acknowledge this.How nature became unnatural, 2018-06-28

She said she had been writing and talking about this for more than ten years "without much resonance." Then James Wells, a particle physicist and professor at the University of Michigan, posted a paper on the arXiv making the same point with a few simple examples. "In contrast to me," she wrote, "Wells isn't a no-one." Then came the coldest line on the blog:

So, now that a man has said it, I hope physicists will listen.How nature became unnatural, 2018-06-28

The same post shows a bigger worry. She gave another example: many physicists believe experiments have ruled out hidden-variable explanations of quantum mechanics, when they have only ruled out certain local hidden-variable models. What frustrated her was not that scientists make mistakes. It was that once enough people adopt a mistake, it can't be corrected, because if everyone says it, it is safe for anyone to repeat it. That leads to her second idea.

2. Stagnation is a sociological problem: until experiments arrive, only peers can judge peers

The 2008 essay is her earliest and most complete sociological diagnosis. She starts with a contrast. A free market works because buyers judge goods directly: you find out quickly whether a candy bar is good, and a price emerges from supply and demand. That is why some people call science a "marketplace of ideas." She argues the analogy fails in three places.

First, scientists mostly chase attention from their peers, not money, although these days funding is tied to attention. Second, a market assumes a product has a value that can be identified simply, but in theoretical physics a research program takes five to ten years to develop and decades to test. Third, and worst, until that point the only way to judge a direction is peer review, which takes expert knowledge. So scientists are both the sellers and the buyers.

That makes the system fragile. She named three pressures. The first is money: people go where the money goes, money goes where the people go, and interest follows both, "a perfect setting to produce bubbles of nothing." The second is peer pressure: specialized subfields tend to misjudge their own weaknesses and ignore criticism from outsiders. The third is public attention: she cited a survey in which 46% of scientists said media contacts helped their careers. The fiercer the competition and the faster the selection, the more the system fills up with people who did well under its rules and so see no reason to change them.

Ten years later, in 2018, the diagnosis was the same and the tone had changed completely. First came the 14-month sum. Then came the list of everything the LHC was supposed to find and didn't: extra dimensions, pretty symmetry groups, new particles with fancy names. She estimated these failed predictions in the tens of thousands, counting either papers or people times average output, and called that an underestimate.

They were all wrong. Even if the LHC finds something new in the data that is yet to come, we already know that the theorists’ guesses did not work out. Not. A. Single. One.The present phase of stagnation in the foundations of physics is not normal, 2018-11-19

That March she had written a dissection that read like a weather forecast. To know what particle physicists will do next, she said, look at what they did before. Step one is to walk back the earlier claims. She quoted Frank Paige in 1998 ("Discovering gluinos and squarks in the expected mass range […] seems straightforward") and Michael Dine in 2007 ("The Large Hadron Collider will either make a spectacular discovery or rule out supersymmetry entirely"), then noted that by 2012 the line had become "Natural SUSY is difficult to see at LHC." Step two is to say that the current collider just barely missed the new particles and the next, bigger one will find them. Step three is to make up reasons why it should, for instance by defining a new kind of naturalness on the string theory landscape. The calculation then shows that supersymmetry sits just beyond the LHC's reach and within reach of a somewhat larger machine. Her comment: "Who'd have thought."

The phrase “theory predicts” has become entirely meaningless.The Multiworse Is Coming, 2018-03-13

Interestingly, in the same post she said she wanted a bigger collider. A collider is basically a microscope, so looking closer always teaches you something, and no special reason is needed: "If we stop here, the history of particle physics will be that of a protagonist who left town and, after the last street sign, sat down and died, the end. Some protagonist." What she objected to was inventing reasons to talk taxpayers into paying.

Within a year her position had flipped. In February 2019 she wrote that a larger collider was "not currently a good investment." She compared it with the gravitational-wave detector LIGO, which cost under a billion dollars, had a clear prediction to test and, once it worked, became a tool for studying distant astronomical objects. CERN's planned Future Circular Collider (FCC), she wrote, would cost twenty times as much, had no clear discovery target and was self-referential: "A particle collider tells you more about particle collisions." She also published an op-ed in the New York Times.

She then wrote up the reactions from the following week. She'd been called a "troll" and a "liar" and accused of envy, sloth, narcissism and populism. After a colloquium at the University of Giessen, an agitated young man said that "everyone here agrees" the failed predictions and the hype were a problem. But how could she go and declare publicly that scientists can't be trusted?

You see, the issue they have isn’t that I say particle physics has a problem. Because that’s obvious to everyone who ever had anything to do with the field. The issue is that I publicly say it.Particle physicists surprised to find I am not their cheer-leader, 2019-02-02

That line completes the 2008 theory. In a closed evaluation loop, criticism isn't scarce; insiders already know where the problems are. What is scarce is taking the criticism outside the loop, to the people who pay. From then on she changed her approach. She ended the post: "But I'm also a writer. Words are my weapons."

In October 2019 she extended the argument beyond physics. As experiments get more expensive, there are fewer of them. Fewer experiments mean fewer chance discoveries, and without those discoveries the technology that would make experiments cheaper never arrives. The only way out of this loop is to choose carefully which hypotheses to test. Yet physicists still work by the nineteenth-century "just look" approach and scoff at the philosophy and sociology of science.

The foundations of physics are the canary in the coal mine.The crisis in physics is not only about physics, 2019-10-30

She saw the same thing starting in medicine and ecology. Small drug trials have run their course and can only find obvious correlations that hold for most people. The future lies in large, long, very expensive studies, so few of them can be run, and they have to be chosen carefully.

3. What to do instead: fix contradictions, not ugliness, and only where you can measure

The criticism she hates most is "all you do is complain; you have nothing better to offer." In 2018 her first answer was:

Consider I was criticizing not a group of physicists, but a group of architects. If I inform the public that those architects spent 40 years building houses that all fell to pieces, why is it my task to come up with a better way to build houses?The present phase of stagnation in the foundations of physics is not normal, 2018-11-19

But she immediately added that the charge wasn't true: she had offered something better, and it came from history. In 2020, when the paperback of Lost in Math came out, she walked through the reasoning again. Historically, breakthroughs came in two kinds. Some came from unexpected experimental discoveries, but the easy experiments have been done, and "you do not just tinker around with a 27 kilometer particle collider," so that path keeps narrowing. Others were theoretical predictions later confirmed, by Einstein, Dirac and the people who predicted the Higgs. What did those predictions have in common?

They have in common that they were based on theoretical advances which resolved an inconsistency in the then existing theories.Physicists still lost in math, 2020-06-05

By "inconsistency" she means an internal logical conflict, not a lack of beauty. Real conflicts include the missing quantization of gravity, the measurement problem, some aspects of dark matter and dark energy, and some issues in quantum field theory. Hence the prescription: stop making theories prettier and fix what is actually wrong with them.

She applied the rule to herself. Her July 2019 post on superdeterminism made her own list of the decade's most-read posts. Her starting point: the quantum measurement problem is a real inconsistency (here she agrees with the philosopher Tim Maudlin) and needs a solution. Collapse models solve it but are hard to combine with quantum field theory, "which for me is a deal-breaker." Pilot-wave theories solve it too, but:

Pilot wave theories also solve it, but they are non-local, which makes my hair stand up for much the same reason.The Forgotten Solution: Superdeterminism, 2019-07-28

That leaves superdeterminism. Bell's theorem rules out hidden-variable theories that satisfy both "statistical locality" and "statistical independence." Quantum mechanics gives up the first. A deterministic theory that gives up the second is called superdeterministic. The idea has a terrible reputation. It is routinely dismissed as a "conspiracy theory," and some philosophers say it means abandoning scientific method, because it sounds as if the initial conditions of the universe had to be tuned so that Anton Zeilinger's brain turns a knob left rather than right.

Her twist is in where she puts the hidden variables. The most obvious hidden variables are the detector's own degrees of freedom. A detector can't be statistically independent of itself, so any such theory automatically violates statistical independence. No cosmic conspiracy is needed; the instrument and the measured system simply aren't independent. Why does almost everyone avoid this road? She thinks the biggest resistance comes from people wanting to believe in free will. But superdeterminism doesn't stop the experimenter from turning the knob. There just isn't any knob setting that would make the correlation go away.

In February 2022 she wrote a long progress report, and it contains the most honest line in her whole method. She has still not written down a satisfactory superdeterministic hidden-variable model, she says, and she admits as much in every talk. But that is not because it can't be done:

But I want to stress that the reason I (and quite possibly others too) didn’t write down a particular hidden variables model is not that it can’t be done, but that there are too many ways it could be done.An update on the status of superdeterminism with some personal notes, 2022-02-26

This is the same logic she used against particle physics: you can't build a new theory from math alone; you need data. Without data you can only guess models, and the chance that any given guess is right is "basically zero." So in 2011 she proposed a test that depends as little as possible on any particular model: repeated measurements of non-commuting variables. That paper "didn't go anywhere." She later learned that von Neumann had proposed a similar experiment, and it still hasn't been done. What she argues for now is this: take small, cold systems, make quick repeated measurements on simple states, and see whether anything deviates from quantum mechanics.

The flip side of the rule is what she gave up. The black hole information paradox is also a real inconsistency. General relativity says black holes have "no hair": only mass, angular momentum and charge. Hawking showed that black holes radiate and eventually evaporate, and that the radiation is thermal and carries no information. Quantum mechanics says processes are reversible and information is never lost. She worked on the problem and, like many others, tried to solve it. In 2022 she explained why she stopped. A set of mutually inconsistent assumptions can be repaired by dropping different assumptions and adding new ones, which gives many solutions that are all mathematically correct. Only one of them describes nature. Telling them apart would mean measuring Hawking radiation, and astrophysical black holes are colder than the cosmic microwave background.

Physics isn’t math. Mathematics is a great tool, but in the end you have to make an actual measurement to see what happens in reality.I stopped working on black hole information loss. Here’s why., 2022-04-23

The problem isn't that it can't be solved, she said. It can't be solved with mathematics alone, and the experiments are "not possible, not now and probably not in the next 10000 years." Why, then, does a headline announce a solution every few months? Her answer has the usual sociological edge. Physicists will never agree that anyone solved it, "Because if they did, they'd have to stop writing papers about it."

Taken together, the three ideas form a single chain. Without data, a taste for beauty passes itself off as evidence (idea one). Nobody catches it because the people judging it are the people living off it (idea two). The way out is to work only on real contradictions, and only on those you can measure (idea three).

How she thinks

Chase the source, even if it means emailing the author. Dozens of articles quoted the Brugger dopamine study from one another; she traced it back to the author and found it had gone unpublished for seven years. The habit later became a fixture of her videos: when a headline says "physicists prove…," she finds the paper and checks what it actually shows.

Replace rhetoric with a count. "Stagnation isn't unusual" is rhetoric, so she counted physical-society members and got 14 months. "Theorists predicted lots of things" is rhetoric, so she estimated tens of thousands of failed predictions from paper counts. "Oh, yes, I love numbers. They're so factual," she wrote in that post. She admits the numbers are rough. The point is to force the other side to answer with numbers of their own.

Keep the receipts. She rarely just says "they claimed." She posts Paige in 1998, Dine in 2007, and Mangano's 2007 slide saying "new particles must appear" … "to avoid finetuning." Years later, when people say nobody ever claimed it would be easy, the record is there.

Use a layman's analogy as a crowbar. An atom in an alcoholic's liver, an Earth weighing one Earthmass, the bears our ancestors hunted, a pile of stones on a trail, architects whose houses fell down for 40 years. Each analogy pries at a single point: where our sense of scale comes from, why a probability needs a distribution, whether a critic owes anyone an alternative.

Change your mind in public. Her view on colliders flipped between 2018 and 2019. A 2021 video opens: "I used to think that we'd be better off without pseudoscience... But now I think that pseudoscience is actually good for us." Her reason was that single-blind trials were invented to debunk Mesmerism, and double-blind trials to test homeopathy. She seldom apologizes for changing her mind. She just lays out the new reasons.

Where she might be wrong

Her prescription is also a matter of taste. "Successful predictions resolved inconsistencies" is a generalization from selected episodes in the history of physics, and she has written that "I may be on the wrong track with this." More subtly, one of her reasons for choosing superdeterminism is that pilot-wave non-locality "makes my hair stand up." That has the same structure as the reasoning she attacks, where small parameters make people uneasy: in both cases, with no data, a feeling about how a theory ought to look picks the direction. The difference is that she always calls it her bet and insists that experiment should decide.

"You cannot trust these people" hits too broadly. In 2016 she wrote that "you can't trust the judgement of scientists whose future funding depends on their continued optimism." In 2019: "You cannot trust these people." By the same logic, a critic whose income depends on audience subscriptions may also have her judgment bent by incentives. Marcelo Gleiser and colleagues, writing in Orbiter magazine, accused her of "defeatism" and "sabotage" and wrote that "our work is to convince society that what we do is worthwhile, even when it fails to deliver the big headlines." We have only read that passage as she quotes it. In 2022 Phil Bull answered her columns in a letter to The Guardian; according to Wikipedia's summary, he argued that searches that find nothing still advance science by testing and excluding possibilities. We have not read the letter itself.

She wrote the sharpest criticism of herself back in 2008. After the section on academia, that long essay glances at the blogosphere, where bloggers compete for visitors, comments and links: "Strategies that are useful are being fast, being brief, and being provocative. I leave it to you to decide whether the outcome is desirable." The same post lists the rule "Don't reward advertisement." In August and September 2026, Backreaction posts carried titles like "Quantum Computing Failure Now Obvious," "I Was Offered Money to Tell You AI Will Kill Us" and "Degrowthers are Dangerous." In 2025, Jonathan Jarry of McGill University's Office for Science and Society and Dan Kagan-Kans in the Wall Street Journal both criticized her for portraying academic physics as a corrupt establishment. According to Wikipedia's summary, Jarry argued that calling publicly funded research a scam undermines public trust, and noted that her critical videos get more views than her explanatory ones. We have not read either piece; we report only that they exist.

Some of her arguments hide a premise. Her 2019 post "Electrons don't think" argues that if electrons were conscious they would need internal states that can change. Those extra states would show up in how many particles collisions produce, and experiments show nothing, so electrons aren't conscious. That works against a panpsychism that gives particles extra states. But many panpsychists claim something else: that consciousness is the intrinsic nature of the physical properties we already know, not an extra quantum number. Her argument doesn't touch that version. The same year, writing on free will, she said that "a large fraction of people are cognitively unable to question the existence of free will." That files everyone who disagrees under a cognitive deficit in advance. It is the mirror image of the attitude she most dislikes, "we insiders all agree, outsiders keep quiet."

Crossovers

  • Philosophy of science · Lakatos's "degenerating research programme": Lakatos said a programme degenerates when it keeps adding auxiliary hypotheses to dodge refutation instead of predicting new facts. Supersymmetric masses ratcheting upward are a textbook case. The difference: Lakatos thought you can't tell in real time when to give up; she says the time is now.
  • Probability · Bertrand's paradox: "Draw a random chord in a circle." Depending on how you define "random," the chance of a long chord is 1/2, 1/3 or 1/4. That is her charge against naturalness: a "uniform distribution" depends on how you parametrize. The difference: Bertrand's puzzle can be settled by specifying the physical setup, while the constants of nature come with no second sample to compare.
  • Evolutionary biology · Fisherian runaway: if peahens prefer long tails, and the sons of long-tailed males inherit the tails while the daughters inherit the preference, tails grow longer and longer, cut loose from survival. Swap peahens for referees and long tails for pretty symmetry groups and you have her loop: judges and contestants are the same population, and the taste being selected reinforces itself. The difference: a peacock's tail is at least an honest signal of health. Her point is that a theory's beauty no longer signals anything.
  • Buddhism · non-self and "monitoring the monitor": her advice to people living without free will, "understand yourself… You are monitoring the monitor," echoes the Buddhist view of the self as a chain of conditions, seen through by attention. The difference: Buddhist causation puts intention (cetanā) at its center, while she reduces everything to particles obeying equations.

Ten takeaways

  1. Given the exponential growth in physicists, 14 months of physics today equals 40 years of physics a century ago, yet the foundational theories haven't changed since the mid-1970s.
  2. The hole in naturalness arguments: without a probability distribution nothing is "unlikely," and we only observe one universe with one set of constants.
  3. Our sense of a "normal" order of magnitude comes from evolution, from hunting bears that varied little in size; the universe need not share that scale.
  4. Sabine Hossenfelder chased a widely quoted dopamine study back to its author and found it had gone unpublished for seven years.
  5. The LHC found only the Higgs; tens of thousands of beyond-Standard-Model predictions failed, and theorists moved their predictions to the next collider.
  6. Until experiments can test an idea, scientists are both sellers and buyers, so money, people and interest chase each other into bubbles.
  7. Insiders already know the problems in particle physics; Hossenfelder argues that what they resent is her saying so in public, to the people who pay.
  8. Successful theoretical predictions historically resolved internal inconsistencies, so work on real contradictions like quantum gravity and measurement, not on beauty.
  9. Superdeterminism puts the hidden variables in the detector's own degrees of freedom, breaking statistical independence without any cosmic conspiracy.
  10. In 2008 she wrote that blogs reward being "fast, brief, and provocative"; her own later video titles are the best test of that criticism.

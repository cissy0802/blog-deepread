Ursula K. Le Guin
Fantasy says one thing: it doesn’t have to be the way it is

Ursula K. Le Guin · Ursula K. Le Guin — Blog · novelist, author of Earthsea and The Left Hand of Darkness · began blogging just before turning 81: first post October 2010, last post September 2017; she died in January 2018, so the blog is dormant (2017) · Corpus: 134 posts in the site archive, densest in 2011 (34 posts), thinning every year after; from 2016 on it is mostly health updates, poems and the cat; about 29 posts involve her cat Pard · In 2017 she selected from it the essay collection No Time to Spare, which won the Hugo Award for Best Related Work the next year · This page is based on 20 posts actually read, spanning 2010–2017.

In the fall of 2010 Harvard mailed the class of 1951 a questionnaire for their sixtieth reunion. Question 18 asked: “In your spare time, what do you do? (check all that apply).” The list began with “Golf.” Seventh of the 27 choices, after “Racquet sports” and before “Shopping,” came “Creative activities (paint, write, photograph, etc.).”

One of the people filling it in was a woman about to turn 81 who had written novels for half a century and earned her living by it. At that question she stopped and sat thinking for quite a while. On the form, her life’s work was a pastime.

She made it the first real post on her blog. She went through her time item by item: sleep, daydreaming, email to family, reading, writing poetry, writing prose, cooking and cleaning up the kitchen, construing Virgil, sitting Vipassana now and then, an afternoon rest with a volume of Krazy Kat and the cat asleep on her legs. Then:

None of this is spare time. I can’t spare it. What is Harvard thinking of? I am going to be 81 next week. I have no time to spare.In Your Spare Time, 2010-10

That is the basic move of Ursula K. Le Guin’s seven years of blogging: pick up a word everyone uses without thinking and ask, seriously, what it is saying. “Spare time” quietly decides which things are real work and which aren’t. The same form lumped “Economic stability and growth for the U.S.” into one option; she wrote “You can’t have both” in the margin and checked no box. It asked “Are you living your secret desires?” She wrote in: “I have none, my desires are flagrant.”

Her best-known claim sits in a short piece on fantasy. Fantasy, she says, says just one thing:

It doesn’t have to be the way it is. That is what fantasy says.It Doesn’t Have To Be the Way It Is, 2011-06

People call fantasy escapism. She asks: who escapes to jail? The direction of escape is toward freedom. So what is “escapism” an accusation of? And fantasy is not “anything goes.” Carpets can fly, but two and one still make three. Down on the bedrock things are as they have to be; everywhere above the bedrock, nothing has to be the way it is. All Galileo and Darwin said, she adds, was that it doesn’t have to be the way we thought it was.

She turns the same move on the word “believe.” Pollsters report how many Republicans “believe in” evolution; asked that, she says, she would have to answer no. She doesn’t believe it, she accepts it, because it is a matter of evidence, not faith. Mixing the two is like saying that if you believe in Tuesday you can’t believe in artichokes.

She turns it on a tough-guy worldview: strip away the “veneer” of law and morality and you find the real us, treacherous and brutal. A veneer covers a solid substance of a different nature, she says; what is that substance? Humans have never lived without rules. Non-violence, honesty and trustworthiness are as natural to us as violence, lying and betrayal. The idea that we are all brutes underneath is itself a fantasy, she says, and the people who cherish it most are decent literary men.

Old, she turns it on kind people. When they tell her “You’re not old!”, she answers that old age isn’t a state of mind but an existential situation. To tell me my old age doesn’t exist is to tell me I don’t exist.

After the 2016 election, at 87, she turned it on herself. She noticed she too had been using the metaphor of war: war on illiteracy, war on poverty, “We Shall Overcome.” She stopped singing that song and took up “Ain’t Gonna Study War No More.” She went to Lao Tzu for another way: water. Water is the weakest thing, takes the lowest path, flows around every obstacle, and never stops going where it must. Reaction is controlled by the power it reacts against, she wrote; acting means not letting your opponent’s behavior decide your own.

What seven years of her blog leave you with is a habit: when someone says “that’s just how things are,” ask who says so, and on what grounds.

Glossary

  • Genre fiction / literary fiction: a common division in Anglo-American publishing and academia. The first means novels sorted by subject (science fiction, fantasy, mystery, romance); the second means “serious” realist novels. The division usually carries a ranking.
  • Fantasy: fiction that allows magic, talking animals, invented worlds and other things beyond the laws of physics, from folktales to Tolkien.
  • Utopia / dystopia: an imagined ideal society, and an imagined nightmare society. Etymologically utopia is “no place”; eutopia is “good place.”
  • Yin and yang: a pair of ancient Chinese concepts. Yang is bright, hard and active; yin is dark, soft and receptive. In the familiar symbol each half holds a dot of the other, meaning they depend on and turn into each other.
  • Pard: the black-and-white cat she adopted from the Humane Society. From 2012 the blog has a running series called “Annals of Pard.”

A strange thing: on the form, her life was filed under “spare time”

The 2010 reunion questionnaire held her attention for a long time. It was anonymous, and many questions were interesting. Question 1c, “If divorced,” offered boxes from “Once” to “Four or more times,” then “Currently remarried,” “Currently living with a partner,” and finally “None of the above.” She puzzled over that: divorced, and yet none of the above? Question 12 asked: “In general, given your expectations, how have your grandchildren done in life?” Her youngest grandchild had just turned four.

Question 13 asked her to rank what would improve life for future generations, and one choice was “Economic stability and growth for the U.S.” It stymied her: treating growth and stability as the same thing was “a marvelous example of capitalist thinking, or nonthinking.” She wrote “You can’t have both” in the margin and gave no ranking. She also noticed that the nine choices included terrorism, immigration and “export democracy,” but nothing about climate, pollution or poverty, and nothing about corporate control of government.

Then came Question 18. “Creative activities (paint, write, photograph, etc.)” sat after “Racquet sports” and before “Shopping,” “TV” and “Bridge.” She wrote: “Here I stopped reading and sat and thought for quite a while.”

What she thought about wasn’t the insult; it was the word “spare.” To a working person, a supermarket checker or lawyer or highway crewman or housewife or cellist, spare time is time not spent at the job or keeping alive, and valued for that. But what do people in their eighties have except “spare” time? She was not retired, she said, because she never had a job to retire from; she had always been proud to call herself a working woman. On Harvard’s form, her lifework was a hobby for filling idle hours.

In the end she found that for her no kind of time was unoccupied. She was free, but her time was not; all of it was occupied by living, tiresome bodily maintenance included. Hence the sentence that later became a book title: she had no time to spare.

This October 2010 post previews the whole blog. A word, a form, an ad slogan is background to everyone else; to her it is evidence.

Who she is

Le Guin was born in Berkeley, California, in 1929 and graduated from Radcliffe in 1951. Radcliffe was then affiliated with Harvard but not considered Harvard “due to a difference in gender,” a jab she slips into her first post. Her father was the anthropologist Alfred Kroeber, and she wrote alien societies with an ethnographer’s eye. Her books include the Earthsea series, The Left Hand of Darkness and The Dispossessed.

She disliked blogs. The word, she said, “sounds like a sodden tree trunk in a bog, or maybe an obstruction in the nasal passage,” and she could not bear the expectation of endless conversation with strangers in the comments: “I am much too introverted to want to do that at all.” In 2010 she read José Saramago’s The Notebooks, the blog posts he wrote at 85 and 86, and thought: “Oh! I get it! I see! Can I try too?” She borrowed one more freedom from Saramago: she did not answer comments.

She wrote her first post a week before turning 81 and her last at 87. In between, her husband Charles had an operation and was forbidden to stoop; in 2016 she posted two health updates of her own. Her cat Pard slept near her knees every night.

What she wrestled with: “that’s just how things are”

Spread out the 134 posts and the subjects scatter: ebook contracts, the Google Books settlement, Oregon’s high desert, Mississippi in 1964, a cat with a mouse, family stories from her mother. But the opponent she keeps running into is the same: a way of talking that passes off a contingent arrangement as a necessary one.

In November 2010, a month into the blog, she wrote about male group solidarity. It channels male rivalry into loyalty to group and leader, she argued, and has shaped nearly all the great ancient institutions: government, army, priesthood, university, and the new one that may be devouring the others, the corporation. Then came a sentence that echoes through the rest of the blog:

The existence and dominance of these hierarchic, organized, coherent, durable institutions goes back so far and has been so nearly universal that it’s mostly just called “how things are,” “the world,” “the division of labor,” “history,” “God’s will,” etc.A Band of Brothers, a Stream of Sisters, 2010-11

That is her root problem. Once something has existed long enough it stops looking like a choice and starts looking like weather. Its power comes precisely from not being seen. Female solidarity, she said, takes another form: not a structure but a stream, ad hoc, loose, unhierarchical, mostly in the private sphere because the line between public and private was itself drawn by men. That is also why it is “all but invisible to men, history, and God.” She wrote: “Elusiveness is the essence of fluidity.”

Over the next seven years she fought this opponent on many fronts. When the Bureau of Labor Statistics stopped counting people who had looked for work for a year and given up, filing them as “long-term discouraged,” she wrote: “They aren’t counted because, frankly, they don’t count.” Kind people who say “you’re not old” are denying her situation. A reviewer who says that stripping off the veneer of law shows us our true selves is mistaking one social anomaly for the bedrock of human nature. Critics who call genre fiction a “guilty pleasure” are hiding a value judgment inside a category.

Her weapon was always the same one too: the novelist’s “what if it weren’t so?” She spent her life writing worlds that don’t exist, so she knew better than anyone that this one was made as well.

Her ideas, told as how she found them

1. Fantasy is a rational enterprise, and escape runs toward freedom

In June 2011 she read an article in the Times Literary Supplement quoting G. K. Chesterton: the test of fairyland is that you cannot imagine two and one not making three, but you can easily imagine trees growing golden candlesticks, or tigers hanging on by the tail.

She saw in it a neglected fact. Fantasy suspends the laws of physics: carpets fly, cats fade away leaving only a smile. It suspends the laws of probability: the youngest of three brothers always wins the bride. But its revolt stops there. Mathematical order is untouched. Two and one make three in Wonderland, “especially in Wonderland.” A story has to start here and end there, and there must have a place on its own map and a relationship to the map of our world. Otherwise the reader is set adrift in inconsistencies or “left drowning in the shallow puddle of the author’s wishful thinking.”

So she separated fantasy from three neighbors. It does not say “Anything goes”; that is irresponsibility, a story that doesn’t add up. It does not say “Nothing is”; that is nihilism. It does not say “It ought to be this way”; that is utopianism, a different enterprise. Fantasy says only:

It doesn’t have to be the way it is. That is what fantasy says.It Doesn’t Have To Be the Way It Is, 2011-06

The statement is playful and makes no claim to be real, yet it is subversive. Fantasy doesn’t only ask “What if things didn’t go on just as they do?”; it shows what they might be like if they went otherwise, gnawing at the foundation of the belief that things have to be the way they are. That is why, she says, it gets two kinds of abuse: “Abomination!” from religious fundamentalists, and “Nonsense!” from pragmatic fundamentalists who want to restrict reality to the immediately perceptible and the immediately profitable.

Milder critics call it escapism. Her reply is the sharpest cut in the piece:

But nobody, except the most criminally irresponsible or pitifully incompetent, escapes to jail. The direction of escape is toward freedom. So what is “escapism” an accusation of?It Doesn’t Have To Be the Way It Is, 2011-06

At the end she connects the argument to science. It is hard to imagine anyone who likes science disliking fantasy, she says, because both rest on admitting uncertainty. The scientist asks how things are the way they are, not how they might be otherwise; but we can’t question reality directly, only our conventions and beliefs about it. So:

Down on the bedrock, things are as they have to be. It’s only everywhere above the bedrock that nothing has to be the way it is.It Doesn’t Have To Be the Way It Is, 2011-06
All Galileo said, all Darwin said, was, “It doesn’t have to be the way we thought it was.”Same post

A year later she applied the same logic to the literary world’s oldest quarrel. A May 2012 New Yorker article called genre novels “good bad books” and reading them a “guilty pleasure,” and said skilled genre writers know that “it’s plot we want.” She answered “Who ‘we,’ white man?” (a twist on an old joke) and proposed what she named Le Guin’s Hypothesis:

Literature is the extant body of written art. All novels belong to it.Le Guin’s Hypothesis, 2012-06

The reasoning is short. “Literature vs genre” looks like a classification but always hides a judgment: literature superior, genre inferior. Drop the distinction and the hidden judgment goes with it. Genres exist, some descriptive and some mere marketing devices, but no genre is inherently superior or inferior. Anyone who insists one genre is beneath the others has to defend the prejudice, and that means actually reading it.

That cut does not say all books are equally good. It pushes the discussion to the genuinely hard place: why is one book entertaining, another disappointing, and another a lasting joy? Not subject, not genre. A month earlier she had been stuck on exactly this. She had read The Help and The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks; to her one was “slightly soiled fluff” and the other “solid gold,” and she couldn’t put down either. She ended without an answer: if one is fluff and the other gold, how come I couldn’t stop reading either of them? The gift of story and literary quality are different things, and she left that knot tied.

2. “Peeling away the veneer” is itself a fantasy

In September 2009 she read a TLS review of a book about the Mafia. Its opening paragraph said the Mafia’s secret world is a concave mirror that magnifies our own; peel away the veneer of law and moral convention and we enter a world of social relations in their raw state, where violence is pervasive and betrayal a common currency; “By looking at the Mafia microcosm, we can understand better who we are.”

She clipped it and kept it, for almost five years, until July 2014, when she had time to take it on “mixed metaphor by mixed metaphor, cliché by cliché.” She named the idea after its favorite metaphor: The Myth of the Veneer.

Her method was a string of questions, like a stubborn student with a raised hand. What aspects of society are normally out of focus? To whom? How does peeling away a veneer let you enter a world? If law and morals are a veneer, what is the solid substance underneath? Does a “raw state” imply some pre-social phase when every individual invented behavior from scratch?

Then the facts. Social animals live inside systems of rules, some innate and some learned, that limit violence within the group, ease communication, and make repeated betrayal unprofitable. There is no evidence that humans ever lived in asocial anarchy. The rules differ greatly, but there are never no rules.

Non-violent, informative, trustworthy behavior is fully as natural to us as violence, lying, and betrayal.The Myth of the Veneer, 2014-07

She also caught the review contradicting itself: it says that in the Mafia world “the natural bonds of family love are defiled.” But that world had just been called the raw, natural one. Where did the natural bonds being defiled come from? The supposed true self turns out to be a corruption of what is natural. Her conclusion turns the charge around:

Rip off the disguise and we are all revealed — traitorous, savage, ruthless brutes. It’s a fantasy cherished by many. Particularly, perhaps, by quite honest, decent, literary men.The Myth of the Veneer, 2014-07

It is a neat reversal. A woman who wrote fantasy all her life, and was often looked down on by “serious literature” for it, points out that the self-styled hard-eyed realists are the ones telling a fantasy, and one that doesn’t admit to being a fantasy.

The same instinct shows up elsewhere. When Pard brought in a live mouse, the classic picture of cruelty, she wrote: “I do not believe any animal is capable of being cruel. Cruelty implies consciousness of another’s pain and the intent to cause it. Cruelty is a human specialty.” We push it onto animals and call it “inhumanity” because “we don’t want to admit the innocence of the animals, which reveals our guilt.” The veneer myth says our bottom layer is beast; she says the beast lacks the evil we credit it with, and that evil is our own invention.

In February 2014 she took the same tools to a more innocent-looking word: believe. The New York Times columnist Charles Blow had cited a Pew survey showing that Republicans “believing in” evolution had dropped to 43 percent. She shared his concern but worried about his wording: four times in one paragraph he used “believe” in a way that implied a scientific theory and a scripture had comparable credibility.

I don’t believe in Darwin’s theory of evolution. I accept it. It isn’t a matter of faith, but of evidence.Belief in Belief, 2014-02

Her reasoning: “evolution” just means change, so asking whether she believes in it is like asking whether she believes in Tuesdays, or artichokes. Using “evolution” as shorthand for the theory, and then saying “believe,” creates a mental short circuit that sets a hypothesis about observed fact against a revelation, as rivals. Priests and scientists sometimes compete for minds, and “atheist rant and fundamentalist rant ring alike,” so between them they set a fatal trap: if you believe in God you can’t believe in evolution, and vice versa.

But this is rather like saying if you believe in Tuesday you can’t believe in artichokes.Belief in Belief, 2014-02

Her account of scientific knowledge is plain: a theory isn’t dogma but a tool. It has withstood massive attempts at disproof, it works, and it leads the mind on to new discoveries. So it is “not a revealed truth, but an earned one.” In the realm of the spirit, knowledge can’t be earned, only accepted as a gift; that is where belief belongs. Her request was small: stop using “belief” in matters of fact.

Put these posts together and you get one finding: much of what gets called “reality” is an unspoken story. The person who says the truth lies under the veneer, the pollster asking who believes in evolution, the friend who says you’re not old: all are telling stories without knowing it. A novelist’s skill is recognizing a story.

3. Yang always gives itself the last word, but there is no last word

The third idea is one she arrived at slowly, late in life, and it is where she corrected herself most.

It starts with the 2010 post on solidarity. Male solidarity is a structure, female solidarity a stream; women who join male institutions “mostly end up being co-opted by them, serving male ends,” which is why she had doubts about women in combat. At that point her vocabulary was still male and female.

In October 2014 she wrote two posts called About Anger. The first was about public anger. Second-wave feminism cultivated women’s anger as a virtue, and she said that was right: “There is a time for anger, and that was such a time.” But anger is a weapon, useful only in combat and self-defense. Abortion-rights defenders hold to nonviolence against the rants and threats of their opponents, who would welcome nothing more than violence in return. Anger points to the denial of rights, but the exercise of rights can’t live and thrive on anger.

The second turned to herself. She admitted that anger fuels many of her acts and thoughts. She dissected her own jealousy: when Hemingway, Joyce or Philip Roth are praised as the greatest she is furious, but praise of writers she likes only makes her happy. So it isn’t jealousy; it is fear, fear that if they really are the greatest, her kind of writing can never be highly considered. Then her sharpest diagnosis:

Perhaps the problem is this: when threatened, we pull out our weapon, anger. Then the threat passes or evaporates. But the weapon is still in our hand.About Anger (ii), 2014-10

Partway through, she stops to address the reader: “You hear the anger in my tone? Anger indulged rouses anger.” Yet anger suppressed breeds anger. She ends without an answer, only a question: how do you make anger fuel something other than hurt?

Six months later, in April 2015, she moved the question to utopia and a bigger vocabulary: yin and yang. Every eutopia contains a dystopia and vice versa, she says, just as each half of the yin-yang symbol holds the seed of the other. Yang is control, yin acceptance. Almost all utopias and dystopias are “an enclave of maximum control surrounded by a wilderness”; good citizens think the wilderness dangerous, rebels see it as freedom. Huxley and Orwell carry the pattern to the end, with yin wholly eliminated: “Everything is yang forever.”

Yang perceives yin only as negative, inferior, bad, and yang has always been given the last word. But there is no last word.Utopiyin, Utopiyang, 2015-04

Perhaps, she asks, we only write dystopias now because writing a utopia would require us to “think yinly.” She tried in Always Coming Home, she says; “Did I succeed?” Her guess is that the thinking humanity is just starting to do, shifting from domination and unlimited growth to adaptability and long-term survival, is a shift from yang to yin: acceptance of impermanence and imperfection, patience with uncertainty and the makeshift, friendship with water, darkness, and the earth.

The last step came in November 2016. After Trump’s election she wrote that it had been one of the battles of the American Civil War; the Trump voters knew it, “if we didn’t,” and they won it:

Their victory helps me see where my own thinking has been at fault.The Election, Lao Tzu, a Cup of Water, 2016-11

Her fault was the metaphor of war. Americans declare war on everything: drugs, obesity, poverty, terror, cancer, even death, an enemy to be defeated at all costs. Even people who know war solves nothing use the vocabulary. For many years her song was “We Shall Overcome,” and she will always love it, she says, “But I can’t march now, and I can’t sing it any longer.” Her song became “Ain’t Gonna Study War No More.”

Here she drew her finest distinction. Self-defense is a reaction to aggression, necessary and justified, but it is a reaction. Defending a cause without attacking is not a reaction; it is an action. “Reaction is controlled by the power it reacts against.” The people who call themselves conservatives now, she says, are radical reactionaries, and the reactionary’s position is the victim’s, seeing enemies everywhere, which is why they have no positive agenda and whine so much. To break the circle you must not let your opponent’s behavior control your own. Which way, then?

Lao Tzu says: the way of water.The Election, Lao Tzu, a Cup of Water, 2016-11

Her passage on water is long. Water takes the lowest path, gives way to anything harder, lets itself be divided and used and defiled, and stays itself; a river can be dammed and drained, but water is incompressible and will not go where there is no room for it; it rises as mist and cloud and comes back as rain to refill the sea. Water doesn’t have only one way; it has infinite ways. She takes the river as her model of courage, a cup of water given to thirst as her model of compassion, the tides as her model of patience. For peace she has no model, she says, only glimpses: a bowl of clear water, the sound of rain, the spray from a garden hose, the scent of wet earth.

From male solidarity as a structure and female solidarity as a stream in 2010 to “the way of water” in 2016, one intuition traveled six years, leaning less on gender and more like a philosophy of action as it went. She had also translated the Tao Te Ching, and this post is her most direct application of Lao Tzu to American politics.

How she thinks

Treat words as evidence. Most of her posts start from a word: spare, believe, escape, veneer, guilty pleasure, discouraged. She doesn’t argue positions first; she interrogates the word. She takes a sentence literally and follows it until it gives itself away: what substance lies under the veneer? Who is divorced and yet none of the above? It is slow reading, and hard to rebut, because she uses the other side’s own words.

Clip it, keep it, wait. She read the Mafia review in 2009 and dealt with it in 2014. She didn’t rush to answer the news; she let a sentence that bothered her ferment until she knew exactly where it went wrong. That is rare in a form that prizes speed.

Put two things side by side. She loves pairs as levers: one book of fluff and one of gold; a resort where you can’t find your house and a lynx with no teeth. Her 2010 post The Lynx spends its first half on the noodly streets of Bend’s resorts and a granny flat buried in teddy bears, and its second half on a museum lynx whose claws and four fangs were pulled when he was a kitten, who was later dumped, and who now eats a grouse sideways with his molars. Only at the end does she find the link: the resorts are semi-communities where people flee crowds in search of solitude and end up with neither community nor solitude, while the lynx, confined in a human world, has brought his nature among us unchanged. “He brings us the gift of his indestructible solitude.”

Admit what she doesn’t know. She says plainly: “I know that to philosophers what I’m saying is childishly naive, but my mind cannot or will not follow philosophical argument, so I must remain naive.” After a family story about her mother slapping a biting child: “If there is a moral to this tale, I don’t know what it is.” On hatred: “I don’t really like going to this place.” Her posts often end on a question, not from modesty, but because she hadn’t finished thinking.

Correct herself in public. When the line “The creative adult is the child who survived” was pinned on her, she searched her essays, couldn’t find it, and asked readers for help. They found the source: in her 1974 essay “Why Are Americans Afraid of Dragons?” she had written that “an adult is not a dead child, but a child who survived,” nothing about creativity. She added the finding as a footnote, noting that in 2016 the misquote was still spreading. In 2017 she chided Portland for not following Santa Ana as a sanctuary city; two days later she added a postscript: “I was unfair in this final paragraph.” Portland had already declared itself one.

Let concrete things talk. For the word “believe” she uses artichokes and Tuesdays; for old age, “that old bully Arthur Ritis or his mean wife Sciatica.” The cat columns look like light relief but are her best thought laboratory: cruelty, innocence, the fact that humans and cats have been working on each other only a tenth as long as humans and dogs, all read out of one mouse.

Where she may be wrong

First, her writing on gender has an essentialist streak. In the 2010 post she says women “on the whole have less direct competitive drive and desire to dominate,” and that male solidarity comes from a “hormone-driven will to dominate.” These are empirical claims that need evidence, and the post gives none. In Belief in Belief she insists that matters of fact need evidence; here she doesn’t hold that line. The same assumptions lead to her doubts about women in combat, a position that is itself heavily contested within feminism. To be fair, her later posts do gradually trade male/female for yin/yang and war/water and lean less on gender; but in Utopiyin, Utopiyang she still keeps the mapping of yang to male and yin to female, asking only that we drop the assumption that yang is superior.

Second, her political posts sometimes miss her own standards. The 2011 post Long-Term Discouragement says the government “decided” these people “do not exist,” and that the real unemployment rate is “certainly over 16% and probably over 22%.” But the Bureau of Labor Statistics itself publishes alternative measures that include discouraged workers (U-4, U-6 and others). The 16 percent figure roughly matches such official measures; the 22 percent has no source given. She accuses the other side of “replacing human beings with categories,” but some of her own numbers are unsourced estimates. The same year, Five Bad Myths compares Reaganism to Stalinism, exactly the enemy language she later vowed to give up. Her 2016 line about where her own thinking had been at fault reads as a retroactive admission about posts like these.

Third, her literary judgment leaves a knot untied. Le Guin’s Hypothesis removes the ranking of genres and hands the question to “what is quality?”, but in The Narrative Gift as a Moral Conundrum she admits she can’t answer it: one book fluff, one gold, so why couldn’t she put either down? Her criticism of The Help rests on the author’s “assumption of a right to speak for people without earning that right,” which is a moral standard, not an aesthetic one. She ties the two together (patience, honesty, risk-taking) without showing how character becomes better or worse sentences.

Fourth, she wrote the sharpest criticism herself. In About Anger she admits that her fury at Hemingway, Joyce and Roth comes from fear of never being good enough: “my insecurity is incurable.” It is fair to keep that in mind when reading her attacks on the literary establishment: the arguments stand, but part of what drove her to write them is the fear she named.

A note: I searched for published criticism of her blog and found reviews that were mostly appreciative, with no signed, sustained rebuttal. So this section covers tensions visible in the posts I read and errors she recorded herself; it does not paraphrase arguments I haven’t read.

Crossovers

  • Primatology · de Waal’s critique of “Veneer Theory”: In Primates and Philosophers (2006), the primatologist Frans de Waal named the view that morality is a thin veneer over a selfish nature “Veneer Theory” and argued against it from observations of chimpanzee consolation, reconciliation and fairness. That is almost the same name and argument as Le Guin’s Myth of the Veneer: social rules are part of our nature. The difference: de Waal argues from data; Le Guin only takes apart a reviewer’s logic, and in the posts I read she never mentions him.
  • Philosophy of science · Gould’s non-overlapping magisteria: In 1997 the paleontologist Stephen Jay Gould proposed NOMA: science covers facts, religion covers meaning and value, almost the division in Belief in Belief. Dawkins attacked it in The God Delusion, arguing that religion makes factual claims. Le Guin’s version is narrower: just don’t say “believe” about matters of fact.
  • Buddhism · the second arrow: The Pali Sallatha Sutta (SN 36.6) says the ordinary person is struck by two arrows: the bodily pain, then the resistance and lament about it. Le Guin’s point that old age is a situation, and that denying it only adds a wound, has the same structure. The difference: the Buddha says not to shoot the second arrow; she says anger has its time.
  • Psychology · Langer’s “counterclockwise” study: In 1979 Ellen Langer had a group of men in their seventies live for a week in a setting arranged as 1959 and reported improvements on some physical measures; it is often cited as proof that you’re as old as you think. The sample was tiny and the result is often questioned. That slogan is exactly what Le Guin rejects: a mindset can change, a situation can’t.
  • Investing · Minsky’s “stability is destabilizing”: Hyman Minsky’s financial instability hypothesis holds that long stability tempts people into leverage until crisis follows. Le Guin’s marginal note that growth and stability can’t both be had shares the distrust. The difference: Minsky is about credit cycles; she is about unlimited growth on a finite earth.

Ten takeaways

  1. Ursula K. Le Guin started blogging past eighty, inspired by Saramago, wrote 134 posts in seven years, and never answered comments.
  2. Harvard’s questionnaire filed writing under spare-time pastimes; she replied that all her time was occupied by living, so she had no time to spare.
  3. Fantasy says one thing: it doesn’t have to be the way it is; it suspends physics but never two and one making three.
  4. Nobody escapes to jail; the direction of escape is toward freedom, so the charge of “escapism” collapses.
  5. Le Guin’s Hypothesis: all novels belong to literature; genres are descriptions or marketing, not ranks.
  6. She doesn’t “believe” in evolution, she accepts it: scientific knowledge is earned, not revealed.
  7. The idea that stripping the veneer reveals human nature is a fantasy: honesty and trust are as natural as betrayal; cruelty is a human specialty and animals are innocent.
  8. Anger is a weapon; still held after the threat passes, it wounds, and her own anger often turned out to be fear.
  9. Reaction is controlled by the power it reacts against; late in life she replaced the war metaphor with Lao Tzu’s way of water.
  10. Her blind spots: early gender essentialism and unsourced numbers in political posts; across fields, de Waal’s critique of “Veneer Theory” makes her argument under nearly the same name.

Blog Deep Read · 01

Kevin Simler · Melting Asphalt

A brain that is always being watched — and how that one idea dissolves most of what looks absurd about you

For eight years Kevin Simler did one thing. Whenever something about people didn't add up — a ridiculous belief, a baffling ad, the "voice" in an addict's head, crying — he refused to ask is this correct? and asked instead: who is this actually working for? The answer is almost always: your standing in other people's eyes.

What he's wrestling with

What a thing claims to do and what it actually does are often two different jobs. Tears claim to be "processing emotion." Ads claim to be "moving you." Your politics claim to be something you reasoned your way into. Simler's work is digging out the real function, and his method fits in one line: don't listen to what people say, watch what they do — and watch for what nobody does.

His three answers

1. Some of your beliefs are cronies. Picture the brain as a firm. Some beliefs are hired on merit (they're accurate, they keep you away from lions); others are hired through connections (inaccurate, but holding them keeps you in good standing). You can't tell which is which from the content — you tell from how your brain protects it: it won't allow challenge, won't take a bet, gets angry when contradicted. He then accuses the reader directly: your view on climate change — whichever side you're on — is a crony.

2. Ads don't hypnotize you; they rewrite what "everybody knows." The popular story is that ads smuggle emotional associations into your head. Wrong, he says: an ad doesn't change you, it changes the map of meanings you live inside — it makes "showing up with a Corona" mean a certain kind of person. So bringing one to a party is a choice you make after doing the math. The unforgiving corollary: there is no immunity. Blocking ads doesn't free you; it makes you the kid who wore his dad's suit to the middle-school dance.

3. There's more than one "who" in your head. Neurons are themselves semi-feral little agents competing for metabolic resources; drives, addictions and consciences all inherit that selfishness on the way up. And the self isn't a part the genes designed — it's something that grows in the right environment. Which means it can grow crooked, and more than one can grow. Hallucinated voices, possession, multiple personalities, the addict who calls his inner voice Stan: all of it gets a materialist explanation instead of being swept away as superstition.

The single most representative example

How does he prove ads don't run on emotional association? He doesn't run an experiment. He counts the ads that ought to exist and don't: have you ever seen a bed-sheet brand campaign (nobody sees your sheets)? Why don't gas stations build an image the way Coke does (pumping gas is something you do alone)? Why does nobody advertise to themselves? A row of absences, all pointing one way. That's his signature move: falsifying a theory with what the world is missing.

How to read him

Start with Crony Beliefs (the one that changes you fastest), then Ads Don't Work That Way (to watch him argue), then Neurons Gone Wild (to see how far he'll go). The blog went quiet in March 2020. Everything is free.

In one sentence

Kevin Simler studies the human brain as an organ that is always being watched: nearly everything about us that looks irrational, absurd or incoherent starts making sense the moment you stop asking whether a belief is correct and start asking what it earns, and for whom.

Coordinates

Kevin Simler took dual degrees in philosophy and computer science at Berkeley in 2004, spent a year in a computational linguistics PhD at MIT before leaving, then spent seven years at Palantir as an engineer, engineering manager and product designer. He started Melting Asphalt in March 2012 and wrote until "Outbreak" on 17 March 2020; nothing since. Roughly 78 posts, by his own count over 300,000 words — long-form essays ranging across evolutionary biology, social psychology, the anthropology of religion and software engineering. In 2018 he published The Elephant in the Brain with the economist Robin Hanson. The whole site is free.

Glossary

  • Signal — how biology thinks about communication. For a behavior to work as a signal, two things must hold: both sender and receiver gain from it, and it must be hard to fake.
  • Common knowledge — not just that everyone knows something, but that everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so on without end. The switch behind a great deal of social behavior.
  • Agency — the property of having goals and pursuing them. The key points are that it comes in degrees, and that it's a level of description we ascribe to a system, not a part inside it.

What he's wrestling with

Eight years and seventy-odd essays circle one question: when what a thing claims to do doesn't match what it actually does, how do you dig out the real function?

His procedure has three fixed steps. First, refuse the "it's just a byproduct" explanation — nature doesn't build things for nothing. On tears: "If evolution devised to make our bodies do something, then the action is unlikely to be a meaningless side effect." Second, stop asking what stimulus caused it and ask what we're trying to achieve by doing it — the first framing "saps the crier of intelligent agency," treating the brain "like a lump of jello that wobbles when you poke it." Third, go looking at the social level for the hidden beneficiary. He stated the method flatly the first time he used it, in 2012:

Words deceive. Actions speak louder. So study the actions.Religion is Not About Beliefs (2012-09-18)

The core ideas

1. Some of your beliefs are cronies

The brain isn't a truth machine; it's a firm that has to satisfy both performance and politics. Some beliefs are hired on merit (merit beliefs — accurate, therefore useful); others are hired through connections (crony beliefs — held because holding them pays socially).

The argument runs on an analogy. A property developer in the town of Nepotsville has to hire the mayor's nephew Robert. Six months on you can't spot him from the outside — he makes spreadsheets, he attends meetings — but the firm treats him strangely: he's terrible and never fired, everyone handles him with care. From this comes the genuinely usable step: to identify a crony belief, don't inspect its content, inspect how your brain treats it. Abstract and unactionable; automatically granted the benefit of the doubt; something you're eager to display; overconfident; something you won't bet on. The biggest tell of all is strong emotion — pride in a belief, anguish at changing it, anger at being challenged. Those feelings have no business anywhere near a belief that's trying to be true, and make perfect sense around one that isn't.

Why isn't this common sense? Because the going story is that people are irrational, and he thinks that gets it exactly backwards:

What looks like a market failure is actually crony capitalism. What looks like irrationality is actually streamlined epistemic corruption.Crony Beliefs (2016-11-02)

The corollary stings. He accuses the reader of holding a crony belief about climate change — whichever side you're on — because for almost everyone no action's payoff turns on whether the belief is true; all the incentive comes from how others judge you. And since the disease is social incentives, teaching critical thinking only treats the symptom: rationality is a multilateral fix, not a unilateral one. Less Wrong works, he argues, not because it's a textbook but because it's a community — people who have agreed to judge each other on accuracy.

2. Ads don't hypnotize you — they rewrite what "everybody knows"

The popular theory (he calls it emotional inception) says an ad pairs a product with happiness often enough that you come to want it. He thinks this insults us: a few arbitrary images can rewrite your preferences? Pavlov's dogs were harder to fool — they at least got fed.

His alternative is cultural imprinting: the ad doesn't change you, it changes the landscape of meanings you live in, making "showing up with a Corona" mean a certain kind of person to onlookers. Whether you bring one to the party then becomes a fully rational choice made after weighing the social consequences. Which is why such ads must run in public: stadiums, subways, the Super Bowl. Search and banner ads can't do this work — the internet is too fragmented, and you never know who else saw it.

The method is the valuable part: he runs no experiment, he counts absences. If inception worked, why is there almost no bed-sheet brand advertising (nobody will see your sheets)? Why don't gas stations craft an image the way soda brands do (fuelling up is solitary; drinking soda usually isn't)? Why don't brands wear different faces for different audiences (because then you couldn't be sure your signal would land)? Why is one Super Bowl audience of 100 million worth far more than a hundred shows of a million each? Why does no one advertise to themselves? Five absences, one mechanism.

What makes it counterintuitive is that it's a defense of human rationality — at the price of removing your escape route:

Ads get us to buy things not in spite of our rationality, but because of it.Ads Don't Work That Way (2014-09-18)

So "think critically about advertising" is useless advice, and blocking ads won't free you either: brand images are part of the cultural terrain you inhabit, and skipping them just leaves you signalling badly — "like the kid who wears his dad's suit to his first middle-school dance."

3. There's more than one "who" in your head

Following a suggestion from Dan Dennett and Sebastian Seung, he argues neurons are semi-feral: their ancestors lived free for a billion years, and while most cells were domesticated by multicellularity, the ones in the brain kept a streak of wildness — they compete for metabolic resources, and idle ones wither and die. So agency is the brain's building material, not its output; modules, drives, addictions and consciences inherit that selfishness layer by layer.

The sharpest step is at the top: the self is not a designed part but something that grew — "the self may be less of a feature of our brains… and more of a growth." If it grew, it can grow wrong, and more than one can grow. Hallucinated voices, possession trances, the two hemispheres of a split-brain patient, the online communities deliberately cultivating "tulpas," the addict who names his inner voice Stan — none of it is superstition to be swept away; it's the same mechanism wired differently.

The methodological turn matters more than the conclusion. He states his stance in the opening lines: rejecting gods is easy, you "just bully them away in the name of science"; the hard task is taking the experiences seriously while standing absolutely firm as a materialist. On exorcism he lands the finest sentence in the essay:

Exorcism is a form of psychological therapy in which the disease is treated as an agent.Neurons Gone Wild (2013-12-10)

This is the same project as his earlier "Technical Debt of the West": Darwin swapped God for evolution, but the Western worldview is still running the mechanical paradigm with the refactor unfinished — so we still look at things that should be seen as growths through the lens of assembly: governments, codebases, brains, selves.

How he thinks

1. He published his own recipe for finding ideas. "Examine the frames that give structure (but also bias) to your thinking. Predict, on the basis of #1, where you're likely to have blind spots. Start groping around in those areas." His own lever is the mechanical/organic pair — using Chinese thought as a foil for Western thought, because "if you're a fish, water can be hard to see."

2. Stylized facts first, theory second. On tears and on the origins of music alike, he first lists a pile of unrelated oddities (tears are the only bodily fluid that doesn't provoke disgust; humans are the only species that sings from the ground), then asks which theory strings them together in one go.

3. Falsify with things that don't exist — a mechanism is real or not depending on what it predicts should be there and isn't.

4. Toward the uncanny, take the anthropologist's stance, not the debunker's. "Science isn't (just) about discrediting bad explanations. It's also about providing good ones."

5. Label your confidence out loud. Right after claiming that "social incentives are the root of all our biggest thinking errors," he adds: "I don't have a ton of confidence in this assertion." The neuron essay opens: "I don't know how much of what I'm about to say is true. All I know is that it's damn interesting."

6. Write things that don't expire. In his 2015 year-end review he credits Venkatesh Rao for the lesson that "it pays to write timeless essays," and buries publication dates and disables comments to that end. The cost is a very low output: he admits that for every draft that makes it out, "there's at least one, sometimes two others that fall by the wayside," abandoned at 50 to 75 percent.

Limits and disputes

The biggest problem is one he wrote down himself. The crony-belief accusation is structurally unrebuttable: "I'm not accusing your belief of being false; I'm accusing it of being a crony. And no appeal to evidence or careful reasoning, or even felt sincerity, can rebut this accusation." A criterion that evidence cannot overturn even in principle has its power and its danger in the same place — it can write off any position, including the correct one, as mere performance.

The climate example shows the cost. He treats "you can't personally act on it" as "this belief isn't disciplined by truth," and so rules in advance that the pursuit of accuracy on every collective-action question is futile — precisely the class of question where accuracy matters most.

Methodologically this is armchair evolutionary psychology. He produces almost no primary data, working from anecdote, introspection and plausibility; writing on tears he calls every hypothesis on offer, his own included, a "just-so story." Of the book with Hanson he lists a point titled "It's not all true!" and concedes that "we almost certainly overstretched our case in a number of areas." The candor is admirable; it isn't a solution.

There is a named, serious critic. In October 2015 Scott Alexander published "Contra Simler on Prestige" at Slate Star Codex, responding to his dominance/prestige division of social status. (That site was blocked by network policy while this page was written, so only the bibliographic fact is reported here, not the substance of the argument.)

The silence since 2020 is itself a data point. Cultural imprinting explicitly holds that the internet is too fragmented and that search and banner ads are "inimical to cultural imprinting" — yet the algorithmic feed manufactures common knowledge at scale by other means. His theory never faced that round.

Finally, a structural blind spot. His engine always starts from selfish individuals and game-theoretic equilibria. It's a powerful engine, but in principle it cannot see whatever doesn't reduce to individual payoff: institutional inertia, the historical sedimentation of power, sheer path dependence. Wherever those explanations are the right ones, he will reliably produce an elegant wrong answer.

Essential reading

  1. Crony Beliefs (2016)His most famous piece, and the one that changes you fastest. You come out holding a diagnostic kit — above all the rule that strong emotion is the tell.
  2. Ads Don't Work That Way (2014)Read it not to understand advertising but to watch him argue: no experiment, just a careful count of what the world is missing. The most elegant methodology on the site.
  3. Neurons Gone Wild (2013)See how far he'll go: from selfish neurons all the way to possession, multiple personalities and exorcism, without stepping outside materialism once.
  4. Technical Debt of the West (2014)The manual for the engine running under everything else he writes: mechanical versus organic, and the line for programmers — "the computer is a machine, but a codebase is an organism."
  5. Going Critical (2019)His last long essay before the silence, and an interactive one: drag a slider to push a network past its critical threshold, then understand why some ideas can live in a city and mathematically cannot in the countryside.

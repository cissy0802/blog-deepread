A Mind That Knows It Is Being Watched

Coordinates · meltingasphalt.com
97 posts · Jan 2012 – Mar 2020 · dormant (last post 17 March 2020)
This page is based on 11 posts read in full, spanning 2012–2019

For ten years he asked one question: the things people do that make no sense from the inside — who are they actually for? The answer was always the same. They are for whoever is watching. And the machinery doing the work is, by design, invisible to you.

Glossary

  • Dominance — status you get by making people afraid of you.
  • Prestige — status you get by making people admire you; it is handed over willingly.
  • Common knowledge — not merely that everyone knows it, but that everyone knows everyone knows it.
  • Free-rider problem — everyone shares the benefit, nobody wants to do the work; where most collective action dies.
  • Signal — an act that carries information precisely because it is hard to fake.

Can you recall a single ad for bed sheets? Can you name one brand of bed sheet?

In 2014 Kevin Simler used that question to puncture something almost everyone takes for granted: that advertising works by planting emotions in your head — smiling faces beside a Coke, and eventually you want a Coke. If that were true, bed sheets should be advertising's paradise. Every household buys them, they aren't cheap, and "best night's sleep of your life" is an easy feeling to sell. Yet you almost never see a bed sheet ad. Same with gas stations: Americans spend far more on gasoline than on soda, yet soda brands buy image advertising everywhere and gas stations barely bother.

What's the difference? Soda is drunk in front of other people. Gas is pumped alone. Nobody ever sees your bed sheets.

So he proposed a different mechanism: an ad doesn't change your mind, it changes what everyone knows that everyone knows. A Corona ad doesn't need to make you love the beach. It only needs the whole room to know that Corona means "relaxed" — so that when you bring it to a party, the thing you're trying to say actually lands.

Ads get us to buy things not in spite of our rationality, but because of it.— Ads Don't Work That Way, 2014

Which means there's no such thing as immunity. Block the ads and you don't become free; you just become the kid who wore his dad's suit to the middle-school dance.

That is his whole method in one move: take a behaviour that looks senseless from the inside, ask who the actor is performing for, and watch it snap into place. He ran it on religion, status, tears and cooperation — and finally on belief itself.

His 2016 essay Crony Beliefs puts it this way: the brain hires beliefs the way a company hires employees. Most have to earn their keep by doing a job — reporting accurately on the world. But the company operates in a town called Nepotsville, where you also have to hire the mayor's nephew Robert. Robert is terrible at the job and cannot be fired, because what he earns isn't performance; it's the mayor staying off your back.

How do you spot the Roberts in your own head? Don't look at the belief — look at how your brain protects it: you bring it up constantly, you're overconfident about it, you won't bet on it, and the biggest tell of all, you get angry when it's challenged. Those emotions, he writes, "have no business being within 1000ft of a meritocratic belief system."

Then he turns the knife on the reader. Whichever side of climate change you're on, that belief is a crony. Not because you're wrong — because there is no action whose payoff to you depends on whether it's true, so your brain was never managing it as a truth in the first place.

The last turn surprised even him: if the disease is other people judging us, the cure can only be other people too.

01One strange thing

In the arid brush of the Sinai and the Arabian Peninsula lives a small grey-brown bird called the Arabian babbler, which spends its life in groups of three to twenty. The groups keep a strict pecking order, and the birds compete constantly — but what they compete over is bizarre.

They fight for the right to stuff insects into each other's mouths. They fight for the right to sit at the top of a tree on sentry duty, where you don't get to eat and you're the one a falcon takes. They fight for the two most exposed sleeping spots at the ends of the branch. When the alpha male sees the beta standing guard, he flies up and shoves him off the perch to take over. And a low-ranked bird who dares to feed a high-ranked one is likely to get beaten up for it.

Chickens compete for an extra mouthful and a better roost. These birds compete for less food and a worse spot.

Simler used these birds to explain why you fight to pick up the check at dinner.

02Who he is

Kevin Simler took degrees in philosophy and computer science at Berkeley in 2004, started a PhD in computational linguistics at MIT, dropped out in 2006, and spent seven years at Palantir as an engineer, manager and product designer. He started Melting Asphalt in 2012 — writing code by day, using evolutionary biology, neuroscience and anthropology as crowbars in his spare time.

In 2013 he set himself a goal of one essay a week. His entire year-end verdict on that: "I made it through February." After which he wrote slower and longer — 97 posts and over 300,000 words across eight years. In 2018 he co-wrote The Elephant in the Brain with the economist Robin Hanson. The blog is dormant: the last post is Outbreak, 17 March 2020. He now does early-stage venture investing.

03What he wrestles with

He became an atheist in his early teens — prayed seriously for about a month, decided the whole enterprise felt hollow, and stopped. For most people that is the end of it. Not for him.

"If the beliefs are false… what (on Earth!) is going on?" He carried that question for ten years with almost no progress, until in 2012 he realised he had been looking in the wrong place the whole time: he had been arguing with the beliefs, and the beliefs are precisely the part that differs between every religion. He compares religion to a magic trick, which always works by misdirection — the magician's talk is called patter, and anyone trying to see how the trick works should ignore the patter.

Move the beliefs aside and the question changes shape, permanently: the things people do that make no sense from the inside — crazy beliefs, absurdly overpriced purchases, crying in public, fighting over the bill — who are they for? Nearly everything he wrote for the next eight years is that question in a new subject. And the hard half is this: the machinery is invisible to the person doing it. If you could see it, it would stop working.

04His ideas, told as he found them

1. Craziness isn't a bug, it's a badge — and then he says it about you

In 2012 he worked out the religion half. Religious beliefs aren't crazy incidentally. They have to be.

The craziness of these beliefs isn't a bug; it's a feature. Religious beliefs are necessarily crazy, not just incidentally crazy — because only crazy beliefs can be used to distinguish members of the community.— Religious Beliefs are Shibboleths, 2012

The logic is the same as sports fandom: precisely because there is no substantive reason to prefer one team to another, which team you support becomes a pure signal of loyalty.

Four years later he pushed the machine onto all beliefs. Picture the brain as a company and beliefs as employees: most have to earn their keep by reporting accurately on the world. But the company sits in a town called Nepotsville, so the mayor's nephew Robert gets hired too — terrible at the job, impossible to fire, because what he earns is the mayor's goodwill. To find the Roberts in your own head you have to stop looking at the belief and look at how your brain shields it.

Then comes the sharpest passage in the essay, under his own heading "J'accuse". Whichever side of climate change you're on, that belief is a crony. He is careful to say this isn't a claim that you're wrong — crony beliefs aren't necessarily false and merit beliefs aren't necessarily true; the difference is only in what you're rewarded for. And on climate, almost nobody has an action whose payoff turns on whether it's true.

What looks like irrationality is actually streamlined epistemic corruption.— Crony Beliefs, 2016

The ending caught even him off guard: if the disease is how other people judge us, the cure has to be other people too. Training one person to be a rationalist just makes them lose socially; only raising the standards of a whole community forces anyone to think straight.

2. Ads don't rewrite your mind, they rewrite what everyone knows

In 2014 he went after another near-universal assumption: that advertising works by emotional inception. His way of breaking it is in section 5 below — instead of arguing with what the theory says, he counts the things it predicts should exist and which conspicuously don't (bed sheets, gas stations). The conclusion is that an ad doesn't change your head, it changes common knowledge: it installs "Corona = relaxed" into public reality, and leaves you to decide whether you want to say that about yourself.

Ads get us to buy things not in spite of our rationality, but because of it.— Ads Don't Work That Way, 2014

3. To understand status, stop watching the top dog

In 2013 he wrote The Economics of Social Status, treating status as money: exchangeable, storable, even subject to Gresham's Law — in an office, the counterfeit status earned by talking yourself up drives out the honest status earned by working, and at that point "only a sucker does any real work." Elegant. But no amount of accounting explains why status grew in the first place. In 2015 he dug again, and came out with a counter-intuitive line he says is the thing he most wants people to take away:

To understand dominance, we need to focus on the high-status behaviors. To understand prestige, however, we need to focus on the low-status behaviors.— Social Status: Down the Rabbit Hole, 2015

The reasoning is hard to dodge: prestige is worthless without admiration, while admiration makes sense on its own even when nobody is seeking it. So the real question is — what's in it for the admirer?

The standard answer (Henrich) is: learning by proximity. He takes it apart piece by piece. He admires Meryl Streep and has no interest whatsoever in learning to act; we admire IQ, beauty and strength, none of which can be copied. And the cleanest cut of all — if admiration were about learning, teachers would be the highest-prestige people in society, and they aren't: a Nobel outranks Teacher of the Year by a mile.

Which brings us back to the birds. The babblers competing to give food away, to stand guard, to sleep in the most dangerous spot are earning a second kind of status running alongside the pecking order. Why do the other birds pay for it — interfering less with its mating, hesitating to challenge it? Because the bird is useful to the group and worth keeping around.

So admiration isn't kindness. It's a bid for alliance. He calls it the Golden Rule of politics: "Admire those who would make good allies." The ethical Golden Rule has to be hammered into us; this one doesn't, because it runs with the grain of our nature.

In 2016 he identified that rule as the minimum viable architecture for human cooperation: the mice all agree someone should bell the cat, and the room goes silent when asked who — and a prestige economy dissolves the problem in a sentence. The mouse who takes the job, "is not risking his neck to solve the cat problem. He's doing it for the glory."

05How he thinks

Lay out a table of oddities first, then hunt for the theory that catches all of them. Music in Human Evolution and Tears both open this way; he calls them "stylized facts". He throws down a string of apparently unrelated, individually inexplicable facts — humans are the only ground-dwelling species that sings (of 4,000-plus singing species, all sing from water or trees; a bird that lands stops singing); tears are the only bodily fluid that doesn't provoke disgust — and then asks what theory could catch the whole set at once.

Treat behaviour as behaviour, not as a symptom. This is his most frequent correction of the reader:

Treating tears as a response to grief is like treating an erection as a response to lust.— Tears, 2014

The question isn't what stimulus triggered it but what this body is trying to accomplish with it. His answer for tears is a gamble that trades dominance for allies: by unilaterally lowering your own status you put your friendship on sale — "Friendship on sale! 50% off! Today only!"

Treat absence as evidence. His sharpest move: bed sheets (everyone buys them, "best sleep of your life" sells easily, and you can't name a brand), gas stations (far more money than soda, almost no image advertising), and the fact that nobody advertises to themselves with a motivational poster. Three places an ad should be and isn't, all pointing the same way: soda is drunk in company, gas is pumped alone, nobody sees your sheets.

Price your own confidence out loud. Right after making the boldest claim in the whole essay — that social incentives are the root of all our biggest thinking errors — he writes: "For what it's worth, I don't have a ton of confidence in this assertion. I just think it's a reasonable hypothesis and worth putting forward, if for no other purpose than to draw out good counterarguments or counterexamples." That habit of stating your confidence on the spot is worth stealing more than any of his conclusions.

06Where he may be wrong

Just-so stories are too useful. He calls them that himself, in Tears. The trouble with functional explanations is that they absorb almost anything: it's hard to say what observation could falsify "crying is a fire sale on friendship." His mechanisms are consistently self-consistent and consistently beautiful; the road from beautiful to true is one most of these essays don't finish.

He conceded the ads essay himself. In his 2014 year-end review: "I probably overstated the case — some ads do seem to work by emotional manipulation." He also reprinted a blunt Hacker News verdict on it — "Slightly hokey cokey with no data or stats to back it up but good read" — and replied: "I think that's a fair summary."

He is often relaying somebody else's radical theory. Music in Human Evolution is an account of an obscure book by Joseph Jordania; he opens by saying "I have my reservations about Jordania's theory (and his book), but I'll save them for a later time" — and never did. The four-part Julian Jaynes series takes the same posture. Readers always get these theories in their most attractive form, and never get the reservations he's holding back.

The knife cuts him too. By his own diagnostic, the theory of crony beliefs is itself a textbook crony belief: abstract, unactionable, and superbly suited to social use.

(Also: the philosopher Tristan Haze published two criticisms of The Elephant in the Brain, one of them aimed at its use of common knowledge. I have only read a summary of that piece, so I record the bibliographic fact and do not restate the argument.)

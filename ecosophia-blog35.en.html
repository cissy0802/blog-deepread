John Michael Greer
Civilizations don't fall off cliffs — they sell one piece of furniture a year, like a man who can't make the mortgage

John Michael Greer (JMG to his readers) · Two blogs, end to end: The Archdruid Report (May 2006 – June 2017, an "eleven-year run" in his own words) and Ecosophia (April 2017 to the present; the name is Greek oikos, household, plus sophia, wisdom, and the site's subtitle is Nature Spirituality in the Twilight of the Industrial Age) · Credentials: past Grand Archdruid of the Ancient Order of Druids in America, twelve years in the post, head of the Druidical Order of the Golden Dawn, author of more than thirty books, and a working astrologer · He has moved three times across the record: Ashland, Oregon in 2006, Cumberland, Maryland through the Archdruid Report years, and East Providence, Rhode Island from 2023, with his wife Sara · Still actively publishing: Ecosophia runs two posts a week plus a standing "Magic Monday" question thread, with posts up to October 2026 · Entirely free, no paywall; the comment threads are enormous (the posts read for this page carry 171, 279, 452 and 531 comments respectively) · This page is built on 18 posts read in full, spanning 2005 to May 2025: 6 of them from the earliest period (2005–2006, inside the first 20 percent of his writing life), with both sides of the June 2017 move covered (three posts from January 2016 and the closing notice of June 2017 on the Archdruid Report side; two 2023 posts and one from 2025 on the Ecosophia side) · Selection was ordered primarily by the essays he himself puts forward on his Blogs, Essays, FAQs page, where he states that "a few of my older online essays have become harder to find than they once were" (the author's own answer to "what should I read" outranks popularity), then by the contents of the anthology he personally selected, Collapse Now and Avoid the Rush: The Best of The Archdruid Report (2015), then by comment counts and outside attention · Two things have to be stated plainly. First, the six hundred-odd posts of the Archdruid Report's eleven years were taken off the web after he closed the blog in 2017 and reissued as books by Founders House; the original site now holds nothing but the closing notice, so every Archdruid Report passage quoted here was read in the Internet Archive and the source links point there. On 21 June 2017, a blog that had run for eleven years and posted every Wednesday without fail put up a four-line notice and shut down. The six hundred-odd essays then vanished from the web — the author pulled them back and handed them to a publisher to be printed as books.

Which produced something hard not to look at twice. The man who spent two decades giving the most systematic account anywhere of how an industrial civilization slowly loses its knowledge and its infrastructure now has his own eleven years of writing readable only inside a web archive.

His name is John Michael Greer. He spent twelve years as Grand Archdruid of the Ancient Order of Druids in America, has written more than thirty books, and works as an astrologer on the side. He lives in Rhode Island with his wife Sara. For twenty years he has argued one thing: the collapse you are waiting for is not coming, because it already arrived, and it looks nothing like what you pictured.

His central idea fits inside one metaphor, and the metaphor is the one he chose himself in 2006. First, how he had tried to say it before. In 2005 he wrote an academic paper, "How Civilizations Fall: A Theory of Catabolic Collapse," and submitted it to a peer-reviewed journal in human ecology. He reported the outcome on his own blog:

In its original form, though, it bristles with equations, footnotes, and all the other impedimenta of the modern academic paper -- it was intended for publication in a peer-reviewed journal in the field of human ecology, a destiny it hasn't yet managed to achieve.On Catabolic Collapse, 2006-05-31

So he threw out the equations and substituted a house.

Picture someone who buys a home far beyond his income. Mortgage, maintenance and repairs together exceed the paycheck. At first he covers the gap by refinancing and pulling out the equity. Once interest rates stop falling and prices stop rising, that road closes. Then he has exactly three options: borrow more, and the bills go up; skip the maintenance, and the house starts falling apart, and the bills go up; or stop paying some bills, and the house becomes less livable, and the bills go up. Eventually he can't make the mortgage or the property taxes, and the house is gone.

That's catabolic collapse in a nutshell. Like suburban mansions, civilizations are complex, expensive, fragile things.Ibid.

A civilization has to maintain a whole series of capital stocks: physical (buildings), human (trained workers), information (agricultural knowledge), social (market systems). If the upkeep stays inside the "monthly budget" that the natural world and the labour force provide, it can last a very long time. But civilizations tend to build their capital stocks up to levels that can't be maintained — every king wants a bigger palace than the last one. And then they are in the homeowner's bind: keeping themselves going in the present by denying themselves a future.

Here comes the fork that makes this sharper than the usual decline-and-fall thesis. If your core resources are sustainable, the collapse has a floor. Ancient Egypt and imperial China ran on ecologically sound agriculture; the annual harvest put a floor under the process, and they cycled through expansion, overshoot and self-limiting collapse many times over. If your core resources are not sustainable, there is no floor. The Classic Maya lived off the fragile fertility of tropical soils, and nature's collection agencies left ruins in the Yucatán. Industrial civilization's capital stocks rest on winnings from a geological lottery — coal, oil and gas, millions of years of stored sunlight.

At this point most readers automatically translate all of it into "so something terrible is about to happen." That translation is the thing Greer has fought hardest against for his whole career. He says every line of evidence points at a far less cinematic conclusion: civilizations take one to three centuries to finish falling, and

The curve of decline, to be sure, is anything but smooth; it has a fractal structure, taking the form of a succession of crises on many different scales, affecting different regions, social classes, and communities in different ways, interspersed with periods of stabilization and even partial recovery.Collapse Now and Avoid the Rush, 2012-06-06

Not an apocalypse — a staircase. On every step a cohort of people drops down a level and assumes it's temporary. From this he derived his best-known piece of advice, which arrived live at a conference in 2012. He had just finished presenting the theory and was taking questions, and one of the other participants said something like: so what you're saying is that what we need to do, individually, is to go through collapse right away.

"Exactly," I said. "Collapse now, and avoid the rush."Ibid.

The method is plain: work out how you would live after the next wave of crisis hits, and to the extent you can, start living that way now. Get out of debt, make your housing cost far less than you can afford, pick up practical skills that meet some of your own needs and can be bartered. He deliberately blocks the most popular fantasy: for the vast majority of people this does not mean buying a survival homestead in the country — "that's a popular daydream," and without the dizzyingly complex skill set needed to run a viable farm it isn't a realistic option at all.

He is no gentler with the more respectable alternatives. On the people who argue online that thorium reactors or algal biodiesel will fix everything, he wrote the best line on this page:

As Zen masters like to say, talk does not cook the rice, and blog posts do not build reactors.Ibid.

A man who makes his living writing blog posts writing that sentence is one reason this site is willing to spend a whole page on him.

But stop there and all you have read is an energy pessimist. Greer's genuinely original move is elsewhere, and he found it early. One morning around 2004, mid-meditation in a creekside meadow in the Oregon Cascades, three sentences surfaced in his head. He brooded on them for a year and a half before writing anything:

Knowing many stories is wisdom.
Knowing no stories is ignorance.
Knowing only one story is death.Knowing Only One Story, 2006-05-24

Traditional stories, he says, have a property modern readers rarely notice: no two of them have the same moral. Think of the fairy tales you grew up with. Breaking a prohibition sometimes brings success ("Jack and the Beanstalk") and sometimes disaster ("Sleeping Beauty"). Victory sometimes goes to the humble and patient ("Cinderella") and sometimes to whoever was willing to try the impossible ("Puss in Boots"). The contradictions are the source of the power: with a big enough stock of stories, whatever the world throws at you, you can probably find a pattern that makes sense of it.

Over the last few centuries, especially in the industrial West, that multiple-narrative habit gave way to a way of thinking that privileges a single story. People who know only one story have three symptoms, and he counts them off. First, whatever the problem, the solution is always the same one — surrender to Jesus, proletarian revolution, the free market, more scientific research, elect Democrats, elect Republicans. Second, because the universe is a complex system, that guarantees repeated failure. Third, failure is a gift because you can learn from it, but when the gift is too painful to accept, the cheap way out is rage. His account of American political anger is that third symptom: both parties had their narratives tested against reality and found wanting, and anger costs less than rethinking.

The real edge on this tool shows when he turns it against collapse narratives themselves. In 2023 he gave a clean demonstration. He set the French metaphysician René Guénon — for whom history is a long fall from a golden age into the present "reign of quantity" — against a set of progress writers like Jean Gebser and Ken Wilber, for whom history is a climb up from Eden. The two camps have been arguing for a century. Greer's verdict:

All four writers are tracing out the same historical process, and all of them look for a dramatic lurch somewhere in the near future which will bring about a world more to their taste.The Reign of Quantity, 2023-04-05

The religion of progress and the religion of apocalypse are the same diagram with the sign flipped: one straight line, a dramatic lurch scheduled just ahead, and on the far side of it the world the speaker prefers. Real history, he says, "isn't the straight line modern ideologues like to imagine" — it cycles, and the particular cycle in question has already run more than once.

This is the key to everything he writes. He manages to infuriate the technological optimists and the collapse-prepper camp simultaneously, not because he stands in the middle, but because he thinks both sides are making the same mistake, and that the story they're making it with is the same story.

  • Druid / the Druid Revival — The priestly class of the ancient Celts, extinct for more than a thousand years, its beliefs and teachings lost with it. Today's Druid orders are not its continuation but a product of the eighteenth-century British "Druid Revival." Greer is clear about why that revival started when it did: it began alongside the Industrial Revolution, because people were unwilling to choose between dogmatic religion and materialist science and wanted a third road that had room for both nature and spirit. The order he led, the Ancient Order of Druids in America, was founded in 1912.
  • Catabolic collapse — His core concept, proposed in 2005. The term borrows from biological catabolism, in which an organism breaks down its own tissue for energy: a society short of incoming resources starts dismantling the capital it already has in order to keep running today.
  • Capital stocks — His umbrella term for everything a society has accumulated and must keep spending to maintain, in four kinds: physical (buildings, roads), human (trained workers), information (agricultural knowledge, engineering technique) and social (markets, institutions). The point is that all of them depreciate, so maintenance is a fixed cost.
  • Hubbert's peak / peak oil — Geologist M. King Hubbert's observation that production from any oil province rises to a peak and then declines permanently, regardless of price or effort. US conventional petroleum production peaked in 1970; world conventional production, on Greer's account, in 2005. Note that the phrase refers to a production peak in conventional oil, not to the wells suddenly running dry.
  • Net energy — What is left of a barrel of oil once you subtract the energy it took to get it. His analogy is the efficient one: net energy is to oil what profit is to a business. A century ago, light sweet crude from a shallow well returned so much that companies only had to find the equivalent of 1 barrel in 300 to cover their energy costs; today's fracked liquids, tar sands and sour deepwater crude mostly fall between 1 in 40 and 1 in 10.
  • Wealth pump — His recurring Ecosophia-era term: empires don't get rich by selling something enticing, but by imposing unbalanced patterns of exchange that funnel a disproportionate share of the world's wealth to one nation. The American pump, on his account, was the arrangement that made most international trade run in dollars.
  • Hobson's choice — An apparent choice that is not one, because only a single option is really on offer.

One strange fact: the man who wrote about loss got lost first

On 21 June 2017, The Archdruid Report published its last post. The whole thing ran four lines:

...and I'm now blogging at http://www.ecosophia.net. All of the posts that appeared here during the eleven-year run of The Archdruid Report will be issued in the near future by Founders House Publishing in print and e-book formats.This blog is now closed..., 2017-06-21

He meant it. Six hundred-odd weekly essays came off the web and turned into a row of books. Go to thearchdruidreport.blogspot.com today and the "Blog Archive" sidebar holds exactly one year, one month and one post: June 2017, the closing notice. Click on 2006 and the page tells you "No posts."

Which yields a result absurd enough to be worth pausing on. The man who spent twenty years giving the most detailed account anywhere of how a civilization gradually loses its knowledge, its techniques and its memory now has his own eleven years of writing available only through a non-profit's web archive. Every Archdruid Report sentence quoted on this page was pulled out of an Internet Archive capture — his 2006 essay about how civilizations dismantle their own future is sitting inside an archive built specifically to resist that kind of dismantling.

This is not a dig at him; collecting your old work and selling it is a writer's prerogative. But it accidentally supplies his theory with a demonstration at the smallest possible scale. Maintenance costs something, and when the cost is in front of you and the future is far away, almost everyone sacrifices the future. An eleven-year archive hosted free is doing maintenance every day on behalf of a future that isn't paying. A row of books turns into money this month. His entire theory is about that choice, scaled up to a civilization and repeated for two centuries.

Who he is

Earth Day 2006 in Ashland, Oregon. The local Unitarian church held an interfaith service and he was invited to be one of the presenters. A choir sang upbeat songs about saving the planet between snippets of Buddhist sutras, Baha'i prayers, Taoist poetry and a bit of Druid ritual. Afterwards, as he was folding up his robes in the little alcove where the presenters stashed their gear, a kid about eight years old came in, looked up at him and asked: "Are you a real Druid?"

Half an hour later, walking home through Oregon rain, the question was still burning, because the answer isn't easy. The original Celtic Druids went extinct more than a thousand years ago and took all their teachings with them; some nineteenth-century Druid groups claimed direct descent from them, which he calls "their lasting discredit." The real roots lie elsewhere: early eighteenth-century Britain, the Industrial Revolution just starting, and a Hobson's choice left behind by dogmatic religion and materialist science, the two victors in the reality wars of the late Renaissance. Plenty of people wanted a third option that embraced nature and spirit alike, and some of them found inspiration in the surviving scraps of classical writing and Celtic folklore. His own tradition, in other words, was born out of a refusal to accept that there were only two stories available. He finally told the kid: yes, I am. The kid grinned, said "I thought so," and scampered off.

The rest of the biography fits in a sentence: Grand Archdruid of the AODA for twelve years; head of the Druidical Order of the Golden Dawn; more than thirty books, ranging from peak oil to Lovecraftian fiction to an annotated translation of a Renaissance fencing treatise; a working astrologer. In 1998 he joined an email list called Running On Empty — at the time, he says, very nearly the only place anywhere that people were discussing the worrying mismatch between the rate of world petroleum consumption and the rate at which new reserves were being found. The blog is not dormant: Ecosophia currently runs two posts a week plus a standing Monday question thread, with the most recent post in October 2026.

What he has been fighting with

He has written the thesis statement himself, so there's no need to paraphrase it:

The core project of this blog, like its predecessor The Archdruid Report, is the quest for ways to lead a meaningful human life in harmony with nature during the decline and fall of a vast and profoundly unnatural civilization.Dancing on the Brink, 2023-04-19

But the hard part of that sentence isn't "decline," it's "the quest for ways" — because he has to fight resistance from two directions at once, and the people in those two directions each regard the other as the enemy.

There is a documented moment where the problem took shape. The 1970s were, in his memory, the one time the United States took energy seriously: prices soared, there was no shortage of finger-pointing and scapegoat-hunting, but at the same time plenty of people went to work on constructive responses. The appropriate technology movement had its boom decade and evolved an impressive toolkit for conserving energy and raw materials; organic agriculture and recycling moved off the drawing boards and became profitable industries; insulation, weatherstripping and glancing at the miles-per-gallon figures were ordinary American common sense. And then he wrote the bitterest observation in his whole body of work:

Ironically, the success of these gambits turned out to be their undoing -- declining demand for energy in the US was an important factor in crashing the price of oil in the 1980s and convincing too many people that conservation was a thing of the past.Deer in the Headlights, 2006-05-10

That is the moment his root question takes its final shape. He isn't asking whether the resources will run short — that's a question in geology and people have done the arithmetic. He is asking something harder: why would a society that got this right once, and got results, forget about it because of the results? That isn't an energy question, it's a cognitive one. And he decided the answer is in the stories: this society has only one story available, the story is called progress, and inside the story of progress there is nowhere to put "learning to live on less," so it gets filed as an embarrassing interlude from the seventies.

So for twenty years he has fought on two fronts while insisting they are one front. Against the progress camp: technology will arrive, markets will sort it out, hold on a little longer and there'll be a breakthrough. Against the collapse camp, which is his own side: it all comes down next week, so stockpile cans, buy the homestead, strike a heroic pose against the sunset. His fire is often heavier on the second group, because they are his people and because he thinks they are crowding out a real urgency with a counterfeit one.

His ideas, told as the story of how he found them

1. A civilization is a man who can't make the mortgage: catabolic collapse

The provenance matters here, because the provenance is part of the point. He joined that email list in 1998, spent a few years on geological data and history, wrote his first peak oil essay, "The Long Road Down," in 2004, and in 2005 worked the theory up into a formal paper for a peer-reviewed journal in human ecology.

It didn't get in. He disclosed this himself, bluntly — the paper "bristles with equations, footnotes, and all the other impedimenta of the modern academic paper," it was meant for a journal, "a destiny it hasn't yet managed to achieve." The other reason he gives is more interesting: after the paper went online in 2005, the questions he fielded made him realise not all of its readers had been able to hack their way through the scholarly undergrowth to the ideas at the core.

So on 31 May 2006, in his blog's fifth week, he did something a lot of academics can't: he threw out the equations, substituted a thoroughly unglamorous metaphor, and conceded that the metaphor was the real thing.

The house is in the short version; here is the mechanism. A civilization has to maintain and replace a whole series of capital stocks — physical, human, informational, social. As long as it can do that inside the "monthly budget" of resources the natural world and its own labour provide, it can last a very long time. The trouble is a built-in tendency to build those stocks up past the maintainable level: "each king (or industrial magnate) wants to build a bigger palace (or skyscraper) than the one before him." Then it is caught exactly where the owner of the oversized house is caught, with all three exits — more debt, deferred maintenance, unpaid bills — raising the bills. He later gave the process a harder definition: such societies are maintaining themselves for the present by denying themselves a future.

Then comes the fork that lifts this above the usual decline-and-fall literature: the outcome depends on whether your most important resources are renewable.

Sustainable resources are like a monthly paycheck. You have to live within it, but as long as average expenses stay at or below it, you know you can get by. For a civilization drawing most of its raw materials from ecologically sound agriculture, the annual harvest puts a floor under the collapse process — even in total bankruptcy, that paycheck lets it rent something smaller and start picking up the pieces. He names ancient Egypt and imperial China: civilizations built on sustainable resources "cycled through this process many times, from expansion through overshoot to a self-limiting collapse that bottomed out when capital stocks got low enough to be supported by the steady resource base."

Unsustainable resources are lottery winnings. This homeowner's income is a fraction of what he spends each month. He can spend freely right up to the point where the balance drops far enough that his cheques bounce — and by the time he hits that limit, the chance to do anything has long passed. He names the Classic Maya: core resources were the fragile fertility of tropical soils, used unsustainably, and nature's collection agencies left nothing but crumbling ruins in the Yucatán jungle. Industrial civilization is in the second category, and specifically: its capital stocks are supported by winnings from a geological lottery, in the form of fossilized sunlight.

At this point he could have written a bestselling apocalypse book. Instead he spent twenty years arguing against that book.

His reasons are empirical. Across a wide range of geographical scales and technological levels, civilizations take an average of one to three centuries to complete the process, and there is no valid reason to assume ours will be an exception. And that curve is anything but smooth — it has a fractal structure, a succession of crises on many different scales, hitting different regions, classes and communities in different ways, with periods of stabilization and even partial recovery interspersed, those recoveries being equally variable in scale, duration and relevance to different groups.

The 2006 essay supplied examples that were startling at the time. New Orleans after Katrina: the United States had suffered catastrophic hurricanes before and the disaster had always been promptly followed by a massive rebuilding programme. Not this time — the French Quarter and a few mostly undamaged areas reestablished a rough equivalent of their former life, while much of the rest of the city was bulldozed or simply abandoned to the elements. He set the ruins of the Ninth Ward alongside the hundreds of abandoned farm towns dotting the Great Plains and the gutted cities of the Rust Belt, and called them a possible harbinger. And he identified the one piece of good news in the structure: the housing metaphor breaks down at one point, because a civilization is fractal — the patterns that define it at the top also take form at smaller scales. The cities in Italy and China that have been continuously inhabited for 2,500 years maintained urban life through the fall of empires precisely because of that structure: a single city and its agricultural hinterland can survive even if the larger system comes apart.

The practical corollary arrived live at a conference in 2012. He had given a talk on the theory at the Age of Limits conference and was fielding questions when one of the other participants said something — he doesn't remember the exact words — along the lines of: so what you're saying is that what we need to do, individually, is to go through collapse right away. He answered in four words: Collapse now, and avoid the rush. By the end of the conference it was on the lips of a good many of the attendees. Outside that conversation, he says, he doubts he would have thought of the phrase at all.

The reasoning behind it turns on its last two steps. First, each round of crisis on the curve pushes more people further down the slope; as each crisis hits there will be a rush of people toward whatever seems to offer a way out, and as each crisis recedes another rush toward whatever seems to offer a way back to what used to be normal — and the vast majority of people who join either rush will fail. His example is the tens of thousands of people who applied for a handful of burger-flipping jobs during the housing crash, because that was the only job opening they could find. Second, the way to avoid the rush is therefore simple: figure out how you will be able to live after the next wave hits, and to the extent you can, start living that way now — get out of debt, make your housing cost much less than you can afford, pick up practical skills that meet some of your own needs and open opportunities for barter.

What he blocks off is worth noting, because it is his own circle's favourite fantasy. For the vast majority of people, collapsing now does not mean buying a survival homestead somewhere off in the country: that's a popular daydream, and in some well-off circles a popular way to have a midlife crisis, but even if you have the funds — and most of us don't — without the dizzyingly complex skill set needed to run a viable farm it is not a realistic option. In all likelihood you'll be experiencing the next round of crises where you are right now, so the logical place to have your personal collapse is right there, with the people you know and the resources you have to hand.

He is equally unsparing with the loftier-sounding options, and precise about the distinction: he has no quarrel with people actually doing something concrete — the ones whose enthusiasm for the Bussard fusion reactor leads them to build a prototype in their basement — but he says that is rarely what the approach entails; it seems to consist mostly of posting long screeds on the internet insisting that thorium reactors or algal biodiesel will solve all our energy problems. Hence the Zen verdict:

As Zen masters like to say, talk does not cook the rice, and blog posts do not build reactors.Collapse Now and Avoid the Rush, 2012-06-06

And he nails one more comfortable posture to the wall in passing: people who donate to environmental causes and put solar panels on their roofs are doing it in the same spirit that led medieval Christians to buy high-priced indulgences from the Church to cancel out their sins.

2. Knowing only one story is death — and progress and apocalypse are one story

This idea wasn't reasoned out. One morning around 2004 he was doing a dawn meditation in a creekside meadow in the Oregon Cascades — sun just beginning to burn through the mist, the noise of the breakfast crew back at camp drowned out by birdsong and running water — and three sentences whispered themselves in the silence inside his head. He brooded on them for a year and a half before writing anything.

When he did write, the first thing he did was go looking for physical evidence, and he looked in a clever place: he went and counted the morals of traditional stories. We think with stories, fitting the "blooming, buzzing confusion" of the universe around us into narrative patterns that make the world make sense. And the most striking thing about old stories is that no two of them have the same moral — breaking a prohibition brings success in "Jack and the Beanstalk" and disaster in "Sleeping Beauty"; victory goes to the humble and patient in "Cinderella" and to whoever will try the impossible in "Puss in Boots." Those differences are the source of great power: with a wealth of stories to think with, whatever the world throws at you, odds are you can find a narrative pattern that makes sense of it.

Then he supplies the test that makes this usable: think of any currently popular political or religious ideology, and you'll likely find at its centre the claim that one and only one story explains everything in the world. For fundamentalist Christians it's Fall and Redemption ending in the Second Coming; for Marxists the very similar story of dialectical materialism ending in the dictatorship of the proletariat; and for rationalists, neoconservatives, most scientists and a fair number of ordinary people in the developed world, the story is called progress.

Three symptoms, each leading to the next. First, the certainty that whatever problem comes up it has the same solution — surrender to Jesus, proletarian revolution, the free market, more research, elect our party. Second, the fact that the universe is what ecologists call a complex system, where feedback loops and unexpected consequences make a mockery of simplistic attempts to predict effects from causes — so the second symptom is repeated failure. His example is the set of reforms the World Bank and the IMF pressed on governments everywhere as the one and only cure: wherever they were fully implemented the result was economic and social disaster (he names East Asia in the late 1980s, Russia and Latin America in the 1990s), and the devastated countries returned to prosperity only after reversing them. Here he inserts a distinction that is easy to miss: if you know no stories at all you may still get by — you lack the resources of story-wisdom, but you can still judge the situation on its own merits and act accordingly, you have flexibility. The dangerous case is not ignorance; it's having exactly one.

The third symptom is rage, and his account of it is the best thing in the essay. Failure is a gift because it offers the opportunity for learning, but if the gift is too emotionally difficult to accept, the easy way out is to take refuge in rage:

When we get angry with people who disagree with us about politics or religion, I'm coming to think, what really angers us is the fact that our one story doesn't fit the universe everywhere and always, and those who disagree with us simply remind us of that uncomfortable fact.Knowing Only One Story, 2006-05-24

The sharpest use of this tool he saved for seventeen years later. In 2023, running a long Ecosophia series on enchantment and disenchantment in history, he read René Guénon's The Reign of Quantity and the Signs of the Times. Guénon's picture of history is the one in Hesiod and the Hindu Puranas: gold, silver, bronze, heroic, iron, with us in the last and worst stage, the Kali Yuga, waiting for the cycle to end and a new golden age to begin. Greer calls him "unquestionably a genius" who wrote "some of the most remarkably insightful books on metaphysics ever penned" — and also awards him a title: epic jerk.

The verdict comes after. He put Guénon beside the three progress writers he had discussed in previous weeks and noticed something: Guénon's "dissolution" — the walls of reality breaking apart, evil influences streaming in from the lower dimensions of the Unseen — and Gebser's "transparency," the mark of the arrival of integral consciousness, are the same process. Gebser cheered it on because he thought it would bring heaven on earth; Guénon denounced it because he saw it leading to the opposite. And then:

Like them, he presents history as a more or less linear process leading from an initial state through the present day toward an imminent breakthrough into an eagerly anticipated state to come. [...] All four writers are tracing out the same historical process, and all of them look for a dramatic lurch somewhere in the near future which will bring about a world more to their taste.The Reign of Quantity, 2023-04-05

The only difference lies purely in the value judgments placed on those states. The three believers in progress see the process as a journey Up From Eden; Guénon sees it as an unavoidable but regrettable fall from a vanished golden age — but all four are tracing the same line.

His alternative is in the last paragraph of the same essay, and it isn't a slogan: history isn't the straight line modern ideologues like to imagine. The movement from quality to quantity — which is also Owen Barfield's movement from participation to nonparticipatory consciousness, and Max Weber's movement from enchantment to disenchantment — has already taken place more than once in human history. That is an empirical claim, not a posture: if the same stretch of road has been walked several times before, it is neither a destination nor a novelty, and "it all comes down next week" and "hold on and there'll be a breakthrough" fail simultaneously.

This is the key to reading him. He can enrage the technological optimists and the apocalypse-prepping stockpilers at the same time not because he is fence-sitting, but because he thinks both sides are making the same mistake with the same story. You may well disagree with him, but first you have to see that he isn't picking a side between two positions — he is refusing the graph paper they share.

3. Narratives are spells: Dudley Do-Right and the danger of your own enchantment

The previous idea invites an obvious follow-up: how do these stories get lodged in place? His answer actually predates the essay about stories, and it came out of a chance request. In 2004 David Solnit edited an activist anthology, Globalize Liberation; two media activists, James John Bell and Patrick Reinsborough, sent him a copy and asked what he thought. His answer grew into a long essay, published in 2005, and he declares his position in the opening paragraphs: he comes to these issues from a somewhat unusual perspective — the perspective of a practising mage and initiate of several magical orders.

His definition is operational. He quotes Dion Fortune: magic is "the art and science of causing changes in consciousness in accordance with will," so the medium of magic is consciousness. Then he brings in a scholarly source that matters: Ioan Culianu, in Eros and Magic in the Renaissance (1984), argued that modern advertising is a form of magic and proposed that modern consumer societies can be seen as "magician states" in which social control is maintained not primarily by violence but by manipulation through magically charged images. Greer's gloss drags the whole thing back down to earth: when people treat, say, fizzy brown sugar water as a source of their identity and human value, their resemblance to fairy-tale characters under an enchantment isn't accidental — they're quite literally caught up in a spell.

Then comes the hinge of his entire method:

Those who aren't used to magic may find it easier to think of spells as stories. [...] Magical combat is a struggle between storytellers, in which each mage tries to define a common reality in terms of the story that best serves his or her purposes.Getting Beyond the Narratives: An Open Letter to the Activist Community, 2005

And then the crux. One obvious danger in magical combat is falling under the spell of the other mage's story; but there's also the subtler danger of falling under the spell of one's own story, losing track of the fact that it's a story rather than the raw undefined reality of human experience out of which stories are assembled. When that happens the self-enchanted mage may not be able to let go of the story even when it's no longer relevant and another would be more useful. He closes the point with the Sorcerer's Apprentice: if you lose control of the magical forces you summon, you're in trouble. Something of this sort, he says, seems to have happened in large parts of the progressive community.

What follows is the funniest and most merciless passage in his whole body of work. He takes Patrick's essay "Decolonizing the Revolutionary Imagination" and points out that it tells a story with three characters: one innocent, helpless and in need of rescue; one sinister, devious and the cause of the first's predicament; one heroic, idealistic and the first's only hope. In that book the three are named the biosphere, the corporate "doomsday economy," and the activist community. Other essays in the collection tell the same story with different names. Then he names it:

Still, you know whose story I'm talking about. It's the story of Dudley Do-right.Ibid.

Dudley Do-Right was a character on the 1960s American cartoon The Rocky and Bullwinkle Show: a Mountie, blond, heroic and as thick as a brick. His girlfriend Nell Fenwick was always being tied to railroad tracks by the villainous Snidely Whiplash; Dudley rescued her time after time, to the sound of Snidely's trademark line, "Curses, foiled again!" The next episode, there's Snidely tying Nell to the tracks again.

The thing he identifies is the grammar of the three roles, not their morality: Snidely has the active role and gets the action going in each episode; Nell's role is passive (getting tied up and rescued); Dudley's is reactive. Map that grammar onto the essay and it fits down to the fine details: "the system" has the active role and is always tying someone to the railroad tracks; the biosphere waits passively to be rescued; the progressive community reacts by galloping to the rescue; and Whiplash Petroleum issues a press release saying "Curses, foiled again!" The next episode, though, there's Snidely tying Nell to the tracks again. And again. And again.

His explanation of the trap uses a technical term from his own tradition, and it happens to be very easy to follow: this is the magic of "assuming a godform" gone awry. In his system, mages choose one of the gods or goddesses of ancient Egypt based on the energy they want to bring into focus — Isis for love, Horus for power, Nephthys for wisdom — and first visualize, then actively experience themselves as that deity. In its psychological dimension this is a way of temporarily redefining self-concept, and his reason for why that matters is a plain truth: who you think you are defines what you think you can do, and that sets the limits on what you can do. People do this less consciously all the time: kids assume pop-culture godforms right and left — look, I'm Spider-Man! Adults do it more subtly, but you'll notice that many of them are stuck in a single image, repeating the same role over and over, even when it's counterproductive.

He takes care to clarify that he isn't claiming activists literally watched too much Rocky and Bullwinkle: like any satire, Dudley Do-Right pokes fun at familiar themes, and we laugh at him because we all know the story he's lampooning. And he grants that assuming the heroic-rescuer image was good strategy to begin with: an effective counter to negative images of "protesters," and a way to impose the image of Snidely Whiplash on defenders of privilege. The problem is that activists got stuck in the role and can't step out of it; and as confirmation bias comes into play, they inevitably see the world around them in terms of Nells to rescue and Snidelys to vanquish.

He counts three costs. One, it locks in a reactive stance: when activists define their role wholly in terms of resistance and refusal, of articulating a NO to the system rather than pursuing a positive ideal, they guarantee that they'll perpetually be scrambling to counter some new assault, trying to hold an inadequate status quo, rather than making the system scramble to counter efforts to change it. Two, it guarantees polarization: he takes the anthology's claim that a small cartel of energy corporations knowingly destabilized the planet's climate for personal gain, possibly "the most devastating crime ever perpetrated against humanity, the planet, and future generations," and calls it "a fantastic hypersimplification of a hugely complex issue" — a Dudley Do-Right definition. His reasoning is cold: global warming's sources go far beyond the mere Snidelyhood of oil companies (they include the actions of the energy-squandering American middle class the essay skilfully dismisses as "soccer moms"), so having oil company CEOs torn to pieces by howling mobs won't actually do much for humanity, the planet, or future generations, while the rhetoric of demonization helps guarantee the issue becomes more fiercely polarized and further from a solution than ever. Three, and this is the sharpest cut: it blinds you to yourself. Playing Dudley Do-Right is, among other things, an effective way to ignore one's own complicity in arrangements of privilege and exploitation, since everything can be blamed on a Snidely Whiplash of one's choosing — such as "the system."

His alternative isn't a sermon, it's a historical control group: he cites George Lakey's discussion of Serbia's Otpor movement. The Otpor strategists deliberately avoided the kind of polarization American progressives embrace reflexively; instead of demonizing the police they pursued a policy of outreach, building bridges that ultimately reached into the upper levels of the police bureaucracy. That paid off handsomely in the final crisis of the Milošević regime, when crowds seized the Serbian Parliament building and the police stood by and did nothing.

But he doesn't pretend this is easy, and the cost he names is one of the most worthwhile sentences on this page:

Of course this would require giving up the very real emotional payoffs of the Dudley Do-right role; the rush of being a rescuing hero is a potent drug, and so is the righteous indignation of knowing your enemies are Satan (or Snidely) incarnate.Ibid.

That is what separates him from the ordinary critic of polarization. He doesn't say polarization is a misunderstanding or a communications failure. He says polarization pays, and that the payment is addictive. Which makes his conclusion far less gentle than it sounds: you don't need to be persuaded, you need to go through withdrawal.

4. Four classes: why he got January 2016 right

On 20 January 2016, with the Republican primary still a brawl, he posted what became his most widely read essay (531 comments, the most of anything read for this page). Two weeks earlier he had made a set of predictions for the year, and one of them had caused more distress and derision than the rest: that the most likely person to be standing there with his hand on a Bible the following January, taking the oath of office, was Donald Trump.

What repays close reading is not the conclusion but the route — because there is no polling anywhere in it. He says so explicitly: the prediction is not merely a reaction to Trump's meteoric rise in the polls and the abject failure of his forgettable rivals to slow him down.

The starting point is a minimal question: how do people get most of their income? Broadly, from one of four sources — returns on investment, a monthly salary, an hourly wage, or a government welfare check. People who get most of their income from one of those four have a great many interests in common, so much so that it's meaningful to speak of an investment class, a salary class, a wage class and a welfare class.

He then does something careful: he states explicitly that these classes aren't identical to the divisions Americans like to talk about. There are plenty of people with light-coloured skin in the welfare class and plenty with darker skin in the wage class; women, gay people and disabled people are found in all four, and how they're treated depends a great deal on which one they're in. His example is specific enough to be unavoidable: if you're a disabled person, your chances of getting meaningful accommodations are considerably higher if you bring home a salary than if you work for a wage.

Then the second minimal question, and the detonator: over the last half century, how have the four classes fared? Three stayed roughly where they were. The investment class actually had a bit of a rough time as interest rates on stable vehicles dropped through the floor, but alternative investments and frantic manipulation of stock prices let most of it keep its accustomed lifestyle. The salary class maintained its familiar privileges and perks: outside a few coastal urban areas in the grip of speculative bubbles, people whose income comes mostly from salaries can generally own their homes, buy new cars every few years and leave town for annual vacations. The welfare class continued to scrape by much as their equivalents did in 1966. And then a one-line paragraph:

And the wage class? Over the last half century, the wage class has been destroyed.Donald Trump and the Politics of Resentment, 2016-01-20

The comparison is concrete. In 1966, an American family with one breadwinner working full time at an hourly wage could count on having a home, a car, three square meals a day and the other ordinary necessities, with some left over for the occasional luxury. In 2016, the same family is as likely as not to end up living on the street, and a vast number of people who would happily work full time even under those conditions can find only part-time or temporary work when they can find any at all. He calls the catastrophic impoverishment of the American wage class one of the most massive political facts of our time — and also one of the most unmentionable: next to nobody is willing to talk about it, or even admit that it happened.

Two mechanisms: the dismantling of the American industrial economy and its replacement by Third World sweatshops, and mass immigration from Third World countries. His framing here matters — both are ways of driving down wages (he inserts the aside: "not, please note, salaries, returns on investment, or welfare payments"), slashing the number of wage-paying jobs on one side while boosting the number of people competing for them on the other; and both had, for all practical purposes, bipartisan support from the political establishment. On immigration he is more precise: the Democrats claim to be in favour, the Republicans now and then claim to oppose, but what this means in practice is that legal immigration is difficult and illegal immigration is easy, creating an immense work force of noncitizens with no economic or political rights they have any hope of enforcing.

Then comes the step he warns his readers will make many of them balk, and it is where the analysis is genuinely original: who benefited from the destruction of the wage class? He rejects both available answers. It has long been fashionable in what passes for American conservatism to insist everyone benefited, or that anyone who didn't has only themselves to blame; equally popular in what passes for American liberalism is the claim that the only beneficiaries are the villainous uber-capitalists of the 1%. Both, he says, are evasions, because the destruction of the wage class disproportionately benefited one of the four classes: the salary class.

The reasoning is brisk. Since the 1970s the salary-class lifestyle — suburban homeownership, a new car every couple of years, vacations in Mazatlán — has been an anachronism: in James Howard Kunstler's useful phrase, "an arrangement without a future." It was wholly a product of the global economic dominance the United States wielded after the Second World War, when every other major industrial nation had its factories pounded to rubble and the oil wells of Pennsylvania, Texas and California pumped more oil than the rest of the planet put together. That dominance went away in a hurry when US conventional production peaked in 1970 and the factories of Europe and Asia began to outcompete the American industrial heartland. The only way for the salary class to maintain its lifestyle in the teeth of those transformations was to force down the cost of goods and services relative to the average buying power of the salary class. Because that class exercises influence disproportionate to its size, this became the order of the day in the 1970s and remains the locked-in political consensus. The destruction of the wage class was only one consequence; the spectacular decline in the quality of manufactured goods and the wholesale gutting of national infrastructure were others.

One more piece explains why the result is resentment specifically. The salary class responds to any attempt by the wage class to raise the issues that matter to it with a double helping of mockery. He hands the reader an experiment they can run themselves, which is one of his fiercest rhetorical devices — if you doubt this, and you probably do if you belong to the salary class, try it: get a bunch of your salary-class friends together in some casual context and get them talking about ordinary American working guys. What you'll hear will range from crude caricatures and one-dimensional stereotypes right on up to bona fide hate speech. And people in the wage class are aware of this; they've heard it all.

Up to here Trump has not appeared at all. He enters as the solution to this structure:

The man is brilliant. I mean that without the smallest trace of mockery. He's figured out that the most effective way to get the wage class to rally to his banner is to get himself attacked, with the usual sort of shrill mockery, by the salary class.Ibid.

Then the most concrete argument in the piece — the one about the hair. The man's worth several billion dollars; do you really think he can't afford the kind of hairstyle the salary class finds acceptable? Of course he can. He has deliberately chosen otherwise, because he knows that every time some privileged buffoon in the media or on the internet trots out another round of insults about his failure to conform to salary-class ideas of fashion, another hundred thousand wage-class voters recall the endless sneering putdowns they've experienced and think, "Trump's one of us."

What makes the essay worth finishing, though, is the ending, because what he does there is not predict an election result but predict the lifespan of the phenomenon. He says plainly it's by no means certain Trump will ride that resentment to the White House, but asks readers not to be naive enough to think a Trump defeat would end it. He sees the candidacy as a major watershed in American political life, the point at which the wage class — the largest class of American voters, he notes pointedly — has begun to wake up to its potential power. And then:

Nor is a Trump candidacy anything approaching the worst form that could take. If Trump gets defeated, especially if it's done by obviously dishonest means, the next leader to take up the cause of the wage class could very well be fond of armbands or, for that matter, of roadside bombs.Ibid.

His closing sentence makes the moral weight explicit: once the politics of resentment come into the open, anything can happen — and this is particularly true, he says, when the resentment in question "is richly justified by the behavior of many of those against whom it's directed." He isn't warning about a populist disaster; he is saying the debt is real, and that the consequences of defaulting on a real debt are not decided by the debtor.

Nine years later he tied the thread back to the first idea. In the 2025 peak oil essay, he argues that declining net energy places a stealth tax on all economic activity, and the bill is paid by lifestyle — which is "an important part of why a family of four could afford a home, a car, health care, decent clothing, three square meals a day, and the other tokens of ordinary life on a single working class salary in 1970, while a family of four trying to live on a single working class salary today is living on the street." The four classes, then, are not a free-standing piece of political sociology; they are the distribution mechanism of catabolic collapse inside one country. The losses on the way down are not shared evenly — they are pushed onto the class with the least political influence, and they can be pushed there because that class's predicament is unmentionable.

How he thinks

1. Do the accounting before the morality. Handed any system, his first question is what flow sustains it and what the upkeep costs. A civilization is capital stocks plus maintenance; oil is net energy rather than production — "net energy is to petroleum (and all other energy resources) what profit is to a business"; an empire is a wealth pump. This lets him say things neither side wants to hear, because the books don't care who is right.

2. Trade the abstraction for a thoroughly unglamorous metaphor, and admit the metaphor is the real thing. Throwing out the equations in 2006 wasn't popularization; he thought the idea was "best communicated through a metaphor." The habit runs throughout: the apocalyptic image of oil running out becomes "that disappointing sound that comes at the end of every root beer float"; industrial society's attempts to "solve" its predicament are compared to "the attempts of guys in their sixties to insist they aren't getting old by wearing their hair in a combover and going down to singles bars to try to pick up women in their twenties."

3. Read any text by asking what grammatical slot the story assigns each character. This is the most stealable move he has, and it requires no belief in magic. All the force of the Dudley Do-Right analysis comes from the fact that he never argues with the essay's moral position; he counts the active, passive and reactive slots and then shows that the grammar itself guarantees the outcome. You could use it tomorrow on any piece of writing that gets your blood up.

4. Look for a control group, not a counterexample. He doesn't say "you're wrong"; he finds a comparison that makes your explanation unnecessary. Otpor didn't demonize the police, and the police stood still at the decisive moment. The 1980s oil price crash wasn't a conspiracy — it happened because conservation worked. And against the objection that no currency is ready to replace the dollar, his control is the early 1930s, when the British pound lost the same role with no successor ready either, and international trade lurched along for decades on whatever currencies and commodity swaps trading partners could settle on.

5. Aim the heaviest fire at your own side. The 2005 open letter was addressed to two activist friends who had asked his opinion. Half of the 2025 peak oil essay is spent dismantling the peak oil movement's own delusions — he says the believers in overnight apocalypse were "quite simply deluding themselves, rehashing Hollywood fantasies and warmed-over Christian apocalyptic tropes under the mistaken notion that they were talking about the real world." And for Guénon, a genius inside his own Western esoteric tradition, the title he awards is "epic jerk."

6. Refuse to prescribe, and treat the refusal as the argument. This is the one most against a writer's instincts:

So what fix did the thinkers we're discussing propose? They didn't. That's exactly the point.The Fall and Rise of Peak Oil, 2025-05-07

The reasoning is consistent: our planetary predicament didn't have a single cause and therefore can't be fixed by a single solution — not some new energy source (he coughs twice in parentheses: "fusion power"), not restrictions on a single pollutant (cough, cough, carbon dioxide), not attempts to rebuild community in social contexts that had discarded it (cough, cough, the Transition Town franchise).

One habit deserves separate mention: his disclosure reflex. Opening the "collapse now" essay, he lists the voices in his study — Toynbee's ten-volume A Study of History, the twin black monoliths of Spengler, Vico's New Science, his college biology and ecology textbooks, 1970s appropriate-tech manuals "brimfull of unfulfilled hopes" — and then adds one more item to the list:

old texts on the magical philosophy that forms the usually unmentioned foundation from which all my thinking unfoldsCollapse Now and Avoid the Rush, 2012-06-06

Writing your most attackable premise voluntarily into your own bibliography is something most polemicists never do. It is also the doorway into the next section.

Where he might be wrong

First, his central theory never passed academic review, and he is the source for that. The 2005 paper went to a peer-reviewed journal in human ecology and did not get in; in 2006 he wrote that this was "a destiny it hasn't yet managed to achieve." The only substantive academic engagement this page could locate is philosopher of science Jerome Ravetz, who in a 2006 book chapter summarized it as "a simple but powerful model of 'catabolic collapse.'" This has to be stated: on that point this page read only a second-hand summary, not Ravetz's chapter itself, so it reports the bibliographic fact and reproduces none of the argument. Either way, a sympathetic philosopher of science summarizing a model and a field adopting one are different things. Twenty years on, catabolic collapse remains essentially a theory on a blog.

Second, the shape of the theory makes it very hard to falsify, and the way he handles disconfirmation makes that worse. His description of decline is: one to three centuries, a fractal curve, a succession of crises at many scales, with periods of stabilization and partial recovery "equally variable in scale, duration, and relevance." Almost any data lands inside that description. And when the most direct disconfirmation arrived, he changed the variable. In January 2013, looking at a chart of US oil production, he wrote:

See the tiny little uptick in production over there on the far right? That's the allegedly immense rise in petroleum production that drives all the rhetoric. If that blip doesn't look like a worldchanging event to you, dear reader, you're getting the message.Into an Unknown Country, 2013-01-02

That "blip" went on to drive US production to all-time highs, well past the 1970 peak, and hold there for over a decade. The 2013 sentence reads badly. His 2025 answer is net energy — not every barrel of liquid fuel is the same barrel, so record total production and shrinking available energy can both be true at once. That argument may well be right, and it is physically serious. But in honesty: this is precisely the move that makes a thesis extremely hard to overturn with data — when the predicted quantity (production) goes the wrong way, you replace it with one that is harder to measure and whose published estimates vary widely (net energy, where by his own account "estimates vary and so do wells"). He may be correct. But in 2013 he did not say "production will rise while net energy falls"; he said the uptick was not worth mentioning.

Third, in recent years he slides from analysis into uncheckable insinuation within a single essay — exactly the move he trained his readers to watch for. In 2025 his explanation for the disappearance of peak oil is that the theory "wasn't disproved by events. It was simply ignored once it became inconvenient to certain wealthy and powerful interests." He then recalls the people at the 2009 ASPO conference who "apparently had a lot of money to throw around," and writes:

How much of it came from the federal budget by way of USAID and similar bureaucratic pork barrels is a question that's been on my mind for a while, but we'll let that pass for now.The Fall and Rise of Peak Oil, 2025-05-07

That is an assertion built so that it cannot be checked, and then dropped. In the same essay he says he has serious doubts about "the way that the climate change issue was packaged and deployed for political effect," and characterizes the mainstream climate position of the time as "parroting the billionaires." Defensible versions of these claims may exist; these are not those versions. A man who spent twenty years teaching people to notice when a story is doing work for somebody ought to be the first to notice this one doing work for him — it renarrates an intellectual defeat as a suppression, which is exactly the structure he diagnosed in the Dudley Do-Right essay: an active Snidely (billionaires, USAID), a passive victim (the truth), a reactive hero.

Fourth, his assertion density far exceeds his citation density. The four-class analysis is genuinely illuminating, but "mass immigration from Third World countries" as a wage-suppression mechanism is presented as settled fact, while the magnitude of that effect is disputed among economists; he gives no figures and no literature. The 1966-versus-2016 living standards comparison has no source either. This isn't to say he's wrong — a good deal of what he said has since become widely accepted — but you are reading the judgments of a very able reasoner, not reading evidence. The reading this page recommends: take his frameworks, and treat his conclusions as hypotheses to check.

Fifth, about that "usually unmentioned foundation." He states himself that magical philosophy is the foundation from which all his thinking unfolds; in the 2005 open letter, his whole diagnosis of the progressive community rests on "assuming a godform," a technical concept from his own tradition. Two things are fair to say. One, he is more candid about this than almost any writer, and many of his best arguments stand perfectly well without granting the foundation — the role-grammar analysis, the four classes, net energy require you to believe nothing supernatural at all. Two, but he rarely marks where weight gets transferred onto it. The reader has to do that sorting themselves, which means stopping periodically to judge: was that step reasoning, or tradition?

The last item isn't an accusation; it's the sharpest line he wrote himself. Opening the 2025 essay, discussing the collapse of the peak oil movement, he writes that "we could doubtless have an interesting conversation along the lines of 'did it fall or was it pushed.'" He raises the question, and then concedes he has chosen the second answer. A thinker's most vulnerable point is often the explanation he has found for the failure that mattered most to him.
